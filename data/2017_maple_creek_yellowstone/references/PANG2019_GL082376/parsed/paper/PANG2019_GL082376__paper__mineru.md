# The 2017–2018 Maple Creek Earthquake Sequence in Yellowstone National Park, USA

Guanning Pang<sup>1</sup> , Keith D. Koper<sup>1</sup> , J. Mark Hale<sup>1</sup> , Relu Burlacu<sup>1</sup>, Jamie Farrell<sup>1</sup> , and Robert B. Smith<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Geology and Geophysics, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, UT, USA

Abstract We explore the detailed spatiotemporal evolution of 3,345 earthquakes that occurred near Maple Creek, Yellowstone, for the time period of 12 June 2017 to 13 March 2018. We generate high<sup>‐</sup> accuracy relocations and near source $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios using 4.4 million P wave and S wave differential travel times derived from waveform cross correlation. The hypocenters can be subdivided geographically into two major subpopulations: a northern cluster with planar structures striking mainly NW<sup>‐</sup>SE and a southern cluster with planar structures striking mainly E<sup>‐</sup>W. We observe $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios of 1.39<sup>–</sup>1.66 in the northern cluster and a steady ratio of 1.50 in the southern cluster, suggesting the presence of $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 ^ { - } }$ <sup>‐</sup>filled cracks. We interpret the northern earthquake cluster primarily as long<sup>‐</sup>lived aftershocks of the 1959 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake earthquake but with some influence of magmatic fluids. We interpret the southern earthquake cluster as a more classic, swarm<sup>‐</sup>like sequence induced primarily by the migration of magmatic fluids.

Plain Language Summary Seismicity in the Yellowstone volcanic region dominantly consists of bursts of earthquakes, often referred to as swarms. The three largest swarms in modern times<sup>—</sup>in 1985, 2008<sup>–</sup>2009, and 2010<sup>—</sup>were triggered by magmatic fluid flow. A fourth major burst of Yellowstone earthquakes occurred in 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 near Maple Creek and consisted of over 3,000 events. We determined accurate locations for these earthquakes and measured the ratio of P to S velocity in the source region. We find that the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek sequence differs from the three previous large swarms in that many of the earthquakes can best be categorized as late aftershocks of the 1959 $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake earthquake.

## 1. Introduction

The Yellowstone volcanic system is a dynamic geologic environment with active ground deformation, frequent seismicity, extremely high heat<sup>‐</sup>flow, and an extensive hydrothermal system (Smith et al., 2009). It has had three caldera<sup>‐</sup>forming eruptions in the last 2.1 million years and has some of the highest earthquake hazard in the western United States (Petersen et al., 2014; White et al., 2009). Owing to these factors, the Yellowstone region is monitored with a variety of technologies, including a permanent seismic network, continuous global positioning system (GPS) receivers, campaign GPS and gravity surveys, stream gauges, chlorine<sup>‐</sup>flux measurements, airborne gas sampling, and airborne infrared surveys (Yellowstone Volcano Observatory, 2006).

Seismicity in Yellowstone dominantly consists of swarms of earthquakes. Farrell et al. (2009) examined interevent times of Yellowstone earthquakes during 1984<sup>–</sup>2006 and identified 239 distinct swarms, which together accounted for 51% of the 23,054 cataloged earthquakes in the region. In that study, swarms were defined based on criteria originally developed by Mogi (1963) to categorize his Type III (swarm) sequences. The key criterion is that the maximum daily seismicity rate $\left( N _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } \right)$ initially grows with the sequence duration time (T, in days), such that $N _ { \mathrm { m a x } } > ( 4 ~ T ) ^ { 1 / 2 }$ . The three most active Yellowstone swarms in historic times<sup>—</sup>in 1985 (Waite & Smith, 2002), 2008<sup>–</sup>2009 (Farrell et al., 2010), and 2010 (Shelly et al., 2013)<sup>—</sup> occurred within or adjacent to the youngest (0.63 Ma) caldera, which covers an area of ${ \sim } 2 , 8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { k m } ^ { 2 }$ (Figure 1). These swarms had rapid migration north/northwest from their initial location and have been interpreted to result from the flow of magmatic fluids.

On 12 June 2017, the fourth major swarm<sup>‐</sup>like Yellowstone earthquake sequence in the instrumental era (since 1972) began about 5<sup>–</sup>10 km east of Hebgen Lake near Maple Creek (Figure 1, red dots). The earthquakes persisted through November 2017 in a rectangular area of \~9 km by 12 km (supporting information Figure S1). In February 2018, after \~2 months of low seismicity, the sequence briefly became active again a few kilometers south<sup>‐</sup>southeast of the 2017 region. We refer to the combined earthquakes between 12 June 2017 and 13 March 2018 as the Maple Creek sequence. This sequence differs from the Yellowstone swarms of 1985, 2008<sup>–</sup>2009, and 2010 in that it is well outside of the youngest (0.63 Ma) caldera and is adjacent to the source region of the 1959 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake earthquake (Doser, 1985) in an area of increased Coulomb failure stress (Chang & Smith, 2002; Figure S2).

In this study, we compute and interpret high<sup>‐</sup>resolution earthquake locations for the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek sequence using data from the Yellowstone seismic network. We compute single<sup>‐</sup>event locations with a regional 1<sup>‐</sup>D velocity model and use these absolute locations as starting points in a multievent, relative relocation process known as GrowClust (Trugman & Shearer, 2017). We also estimate near<sup>‐</sup>source $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios from the cross correlation<sup>‐</sup>derived differential travel times measured for the relative relocation. We examine the space<sup>–</sup>time evolution of the seismicity and the $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios to constrain the mechanism tha drove the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek earthquake sequence.

## 2. Absolute Earthquake Locations and Magnitudes

Earthquakes in the Yellowstone region are routinely detected and located by the University of Utah Seismograph Stations using HYPOINVERSE (Klein, 2002) with a 1<sup>‐</sup>D velocity model (Table S1). Owing to the high density of seismometers in the region, the absolute locations of the 3,345 events in the Maple Creek sequence have low epicentral uncertainties and well<sup>‐</sup>determined focal depths. The median horizonta standard error (ehr) is 500 m, and the median standard error in depth (ehz) is 730 m (Figure S3). Over 97% of the events are located at depths of 6<sup>–</sup>14 km beneath the surface (mean elevation of 2.3 km) in a region of relatively high $V _ { \mathrm { P } } ,$ which is overlain by a region of relatively low $V _ { \mathrm { P } }$ that has been interpreted as a $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ gas<sup>‐</sup>saturated body (Husen et al., 2004). Coda duration magnitudes $\mathrm { ( M _ { C } ) }$ were calculated for all the events in the sequence and varied from −1.7 to 4.4. We estimate a magnitude of completeness of $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { C } } \ : 0 . 5$ and a b value of 0.96 from least squares fitting of the magnitude<sup>‐</sup>frequency data between $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { C } } 0 . 5$ and 3.5 (Figure S1).

Only one earthquake was large enough that its regional waveforms could be inverted for a moment tensor (16 June 2017 00:48:47, 44.7813°N, 111.033°W, 11.6 km, M 4.36). Using the methodology of Minson and Dreger (2008), and assuming a deviatoric source mechanism, we obtained an ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 4.4 oblique<sup>‐</sup>normal solu tion (Figure S4) with one nodal plane dipping $6 2 ^ { \circ }$ to the northeast and striking to the northwest at 327°, which is subparallel to the 1959 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake fault scarp (Figure 2). The northeast<sup>‐</sup>southwest oriented T axis is consistent with the GPS<sup>‐</sup>determined regional strain field as well as historic earthquakes (Payne et al., 2012; Puskas et al., 2007; Schmeelk et al., 2017). The best fitting moment centroid depth is 15 km, \~6 km deeper than the focal depth derived from arrival times. This difference may result from the difference in velocity models used in the two procedures, although the centroid depth is relatively poorly constrained. We also attempted to invert the waveforms for a full moment tensor with six degrees of free dom. We found an isotropic component accounting for 33% of the moment; however, the F test statistic for the isotropic component is significant at a confidence level of only 57%, much lower than the 95% value that is used to accept an apparent isotropic component as genuine (e.g., Whidden & Pankow, 2012).

## 3. High‐Precision Relative Relocation of Earthquakes

To explore small<sup>‐</sup>scale structures in the seismicity, we measured \~4.4 million high<sup>‐</sup>quality waveform<sup>‐</sup>based differential travel times (27% S and 73% P) with a bispectrum cross‐correlation package, BCSEIS (Du et al., 2004), from 22 local stations and 2 regional stations (Figure 1, yellow stars) that were selected based on data quality and azimuthal coverage. We inverted the differential times for relative locations with the hierarchical clustering relocation algorithm GrowClust (Trugman & Shearer, 2017). Before starting relocation, GrowClust fixes the centroid of the entire sequence and sorts event pairs by similarity coefficients obtained from waveform cross correlation. The event pairs are relocated sequentially with a grid search, starting with the most similar pair. When a new event is paired with previously relocated events, it will remain at its ori ginal location if the overall residual does not decrease during the grid search. With our high‐quality P wave and S wave differential travel times, we were able to relocate 3,257 out of 3,345 events with GrowClust. Because of the proximity of station YMC, and the large number of differential S‐P times, we obtained high<sup>‐</sup>precision relative relocations with low uncertainties. Using 100 bootstrap resampling runs, we estimate a median vertical error of 86 m and a median horizontal error of 62 m (Figure S3). The true uncertainties may be slightly higher because of simplifications made in the forward problem such as the use of a 1<sup>‐</sup>D isotropic velocity model.

## 3.1. Spatial Structure and Geometry of Relocated Earthquakes

Based on geographic and temporal considerations, the relocated hypocenters can be divided into two major sequences. The northern sequence, which we refer to as Cluster n, occurred exclusively in 2017 to the NNW of station YMC (Figure 2). It forms an oblique V<sup>‐</sup>shaped pattern with a NW<sup>‐</sup>SE strike similar to that of the Hebgen Lake fault scarp and the northeast dipping nodal plane ofthe ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 4.4 earthquake. On a smaller scale, Cluster n also contains orthogonal planar features that strike NE<sup>–</sup>SW, similar to the strike of the southeast<sup>‐</sup> dipping nodal plane of the $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathbf { w } }$ 4.4 earthquake. Based on finer<sup>‐</sup>scale features, we divided Cluster n into three smaller clusters (Figure 2): n<sup>‐</sup>I, containing the earliest events in 2017, which expanded outward from the sequence origin near the ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 4.4 epicenter; n<sup>‐</sup>II, containing the more scattered events to the NNW that showed northwest<sup>‐</sup>to<sup>‐</sup>southeast migration; and n<sup>‐</sup>III, containing the less scattered events in the eastern part of the sequence with northwest<sup>‐</sup>to<sup>‐</sup>southeast migration. We used principal component analysis (PCA) on the spatial covariance matrices of the clusters to quantify their geometrical structure (e.g., Michelini & Bolt, 1986). Our PCA implementation returns: (1) a scalar measure of planarity that varies from 0 for a perfectly spherical distribution to 1 for a perfectly planar distribution, (2) the orientation of the optimal plane in a least squares sense, and (3) bootstrap<sup>‐</sup>derived uncertainties for the orientation of the optimal plane (Pang et al., 2018). All three clusters are described by planes striking NW<sup>‐</sup>SE and dipping steeply to the southwest (Figure 2 and Table S2), but only Cluster n<sup>‐</sup>I has a relatively high planarity (0.89) indicating a robust struc ture. The lower planarities of Clusters n<sup>‐</sup>II and n<sup>‐</sup>III (0.77 and 0.78) are caused by smaller<sup>‐</sup>scale features (e.g., NE<sup>–</sup>SW striking planes) that combine to give artificially steep dips when fit together as a single plane.

The southern portion of the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek sequence, which we refer to as Cluster $s ,$ occurred mostly in 2018 to the SSE of station YMC (Figure 2). Based on origin times, dip direction, and epicentral dis tribution, we divided Cluster s into five smaller clusters (s<sup>‐</sup>I, s<sup>‐</sup>II, s<sup>‐</sup>III, s<sup>‐</sup>IV, and s<sup>‐</sup>V) and used PCA to quantify their geometrical properties (Figure 2 and Table S2). These clusters were well described by planes<sup>—</sup>four had planarities >0.95, and the fifth had a planarity of 0.85<sup>—</sup>which were rotated slightly counterclockwise relative to those in the north, such that they had nearly EW strikes (Figure 2 and Table S2). The dips were generally less steep than the planes in Cluster n, with two of the planes dipping to the south (s<sup>‐</sup>II and s<sup>‐</sup>V) and three dipping to the north (s<sup>‐</sup>I, s<sup>‐</sup>III, and s<sup>‐</sup>IV).

## 3.2. Space–Time Migration of Relocated Earthquakes

We examined the space<sup>–</sup>time migration of hypocenters to determine if there were patterns consistent with the diffusion of fluids. We calculated separate migration rates for earthquakes occurring in 2017 (Clusters n<sup>‐</sup>I, n<sup>‐</sup>II, n<sup>‐</sup>III, and s<sup>‐</sup>I) and 2018 (Clusters s<sup>‐</sup>II, s<sup>‐</sup>III, s<sup>‐</sup>IV, and s<sup>‐</sup>V). The migration rates for both sequences are smaller than 0.1 km/hr (Figure S5), which has been suggested as a critical value that, if exceeded, would be too fast for a fluid<sup>‐</sup>driven sequence (Chen & Shearer, 2011; Hauksson et al., 2016). The migration rate for earthquakes in 2018 was slightly faster than for earthquakes in 2017. By fitting a homogeneous 3<sup>‐</sup>D fluid diffusion model (Shapiro et al., 1997) to the space<sup>–</sup>time distribution of hypocenters, we obtained an overall hydraulic diffusivity (D) of $0 . 0 2 { - } 0 . 4 0 \ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { s }$ for the 2017 earthquakes and a slightly higher value, $\pmb { D } \geq$ $0 . 4 0 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { s }$ for the 2018 earthquakes (Figure S5).

We also examined migration rates for some of the individual clusters that showed especially high organization: Clusters n<sup>‐</sup>II and n<sup>‐</sup>III, which showed northwest to southeast linear migration, and Cluster s<sup>‐</sup>I, which occurred in July 2017 and showed west to east migration (Figure S6). For Clusters n<sup>‐</sup>II and n<sup>‐</sup>III, we only chose earthquakes occurring in August 2017, which is a majority of events in each cluster, to fit the 3<sup>‐</sup>D dif fusion models. We obtained well<sup>‐</sup>fitting diffusion models with $D \sim 1 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { s }$ for Clusters n<sup>‐</sup>II and s<sup>‐</sup>I, which is close to the diffusion rates of previous Yellowstone earthquake swarms (Shelly et al., 2013). In contrast, Cluster n‐III seems less diffusive‐like with D of only ${ \sim } 0 . 0 2 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { s }$

## 4. Imaging $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ in the Source Region

We used the approach of Lin and Shearer (2007) to estimate $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios in the source region of 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek sequence. Instead of removing the mean differential time like Lin and Shearer (2007), we sub tracted the median P and S differential time in each event pair to mitigate the origin time bias. We selected event pairs with S<sup>‐</sup>P differential times measured at a minimum of three stations, resulting in 177,697 pairs from 2,401 events that yielded a $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio of 1.602 ±0.011. An error of 0.001 is estimated from 200 boot strap resamplings, and another 0.01 uncertainty is the estimated bias from the takeoff angle inequality between P wave and S wave raypaths. Our $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ estimate is within the range of 1.57<sup>–</sup>1.61 that was pre viously observed for this portion of the Yellowstone region from local earthquake tomography (Husen et al., 2004). This consistency supports the idea that the assumptions of the Lin and Shearer (2007) method related to similarity of near<sup>‐</sup>source raypaths are being met.

## 4.1. Spatial Variations in $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$

We next examined spatial variations in the $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio. Considering just the northern portion of the sequence that we previously defined as Cluster n in Figure 2, we have 139,740 pairs of P and S differential times from 1,672 events, leading to a $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio of $1 . 6 2 4 \pm 0 . 0 1 2$

We experimented with different methods of subdividing this sequence and ultimately settled on the three smaller clusters defined previously as n<sup>‐</sup>I, n<sup>‐</sup>II, and n<sup>‐</sup>III (Figure 3). We obtained 236 events for Cluster n<sup>‐</sup> I yielding 18,458 viable differential time pairs, which gave a $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio of $1 . 4 9 4 \pm 0 . 0 1 4$ . The 387 events in Cluster n<sup>‐</sup>II yielded 16,580 viable differential time pairs, which give an extremely low $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio of 1.393 ±0.017. The 930 events in Cluster n<sup>‐</sup>III yielded 95,019 viable differential time pairs, resulting in a relatively high $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio of $1 . 6 5 8 \pm 0 . 0 0 3 ( \mathrm { F i g u r e } 3 )$ .

For the southern Cluster s, from which we previously defined clusters s<sup>‐</sup>I, s<sup>‐</sup>II, s<sup>‐</sup>III, s<sup>‐</sup>IV, and s<sup>‐</sup>V (Figure 2), we have 37,394 viable differential time pairs from 724 events, leading to a $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio of $1 . 5 0 5 \pm 0 . 0 0 2$ (Figure 3). We found $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios that varied from 1.48 to 1.55 for the five smaller clusters; however, the values were poorly resolved with errors of \~0.02 from 200 bootstrap resamplings. In addition, there was 0.01 error estimated from takeoff angle inequality between P and S raypaths. Thus, we consider there to be little evidence for significant spatial variation in $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios within Cluster s.

## 4.2. Interpretation of Observed $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ Ratios

We observe $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios of 1.40<sup>–</sup>1.66 throughout the source region of the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek sequence, much lower than the average value of 1.77 for rocks in the continental crust (Christensen, 1996). Partial melt would lead to above average $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios and our low $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios instead imply the presence of aqueous fluids (Watanabe, 1993). Similarly low $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ observations and interpretations have been made in other vol canic regions, including Hawaii (Lin et al., 2015) and northeastern Japan (Nakajima et al., 2001). The pre sence of fluids in the source region of the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creak sequence is also suggested by earthquake migration patterns that can be fit by a 3<sup>‐</sup>D homogenous diffusion model with hydraulic diffusiv ities of 0.02<sup>–</sup>1 m<sup>2</sup>/s (Figures S5 and S6).

It is difficult to determine from our seismic observations whether the fluids in the source region are liquid water, $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } / \mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ gases, or a mixture of the two. Gregory (1976) obtained $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios of 1.42<sup>–</sup>1.98 for water<sup>‐</sup>saturated rocks and 1.30<sup>–</sup>1.69 for gas<sup>‐</sup>saturated rocks. More recently, Lin et al. (2015) calculated $V _ { P } /$ $V _ { S }$ ratios for a host rock with a $V _ { P }$ of 6.0 km/s, a $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio of 1.74, and ellipsoidal cracks with aspect ratios from 0.001 to 0.30. From their simulations, the lowest $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio obtained from a water<sup>‐</sup>filled crack model was 1.594, but the $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } .$ <sup>‐</sup>filled crack models could reduce the ratio to as low as the value of 1.412 observed in the Kilauea (Hawaii) caldera. Thus, while not unique, our preferred interpretation of the low $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ values near the Maple Creek sequence is the presence of $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ <sup>‐</sup>filled cracks.

Lin et al. (2015) also found that for $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } .$ <sup>‐</sup>filled cracks with constant aspect ratio, $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ decreases with increasing porosity. This provides a possible explanation for our observation of a reduction in $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ from 1.66 to 1.39 between the southeastern and northwestern portions of the Cluster n (Figure 3). The $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ decrease we observe would require a porosity increase of \~80%, which might be expected if there were increased damage in the northwestern segment of Cluster n (i.e., Cluster n<sup>‐</sup>II) because of its closer proximity to the Hebgen Lake rupture zone. Johnson et al. (2018) noted significant scarp height in the southeastern portion of the rupture zone, and because of the relatively low strain rates, it is plausible that the damage in the rupture zone has not yet had time to heal. The increased epicentral scatter in Cluster n<sup>‐</sup>II is also con sistent with a highly fractured region.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. The Northern Portion of the 2017–2018 Maple Creek Sequence

The northern portion of the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek earthquake sequence, which we designate as Cluster n, exhibits a V<sup>‐</sup>shaped structure that mainly strikes northwest<sup>‐</sup>southeast (Figure 2). On a smaller scale, the numerous <sup>“</sup>X<sup>”‐</sup>shaped structures associated with the PCA results for three subclusters are consistent with composite focal mechanisms from Shelly and Hardebeck (2019), which suggest the existence of a mesh<sup>‐</sup>like fracture network in the region. Magmatic fluids likely played a major role in earlier Yellowstone swarms (Farrell et al., 2010; Shelly et al., 2013; Waite & Smith, 2002), and Shelly and Hardebeck (2019) suggested that this portion of the sequence is also related to fluids. However, fluid diffusion alone does not explain the change in migration rates between Cluster n‐II and Cluster n‐III. The low overall diffusivity $( 0 . 4 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { s } )$ for the northern portion of the sequence, the less‐diffusive‐like properties of Cluster n‐III, and the double<sup>‐</sup>couple nature of the moment tensor of the largest event imply that there may be an alternative force driving the seismicity. Several observations suggest that Cluster n is primarily a continuation of the long lived aftershock sequence of the 1959 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake earthquake: (1) The earthquakes are adjacent to (within 1<sup>–</sup>2 rupture lengths) the southeast end of the surface rupture of the Hebgen Lake earthquake, (2) the earthquakes are in a region of positive Coulomb stress perturbation from the Hebgen Lake event (Figure S3, Chang & Smith, 2002), (3) the overall NW<sup>‐</sup>SE structure of the cluster is consistent with the orientation of fault scarps of the Hebgen Lake event, and (4) the T axis of the ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 4.4 event on 16 June 2017 is con sistent with T axes of Hebgen Lake event and one of its aftershocks (Doser, 1985).

We also studied the temporal evolution of Cluster n using an approach similar to Vidale and Shearer (2006). We divided Cluster n into three subsequences based on their origin time and location (Figure 4a and Figure S7). We calculated their daily rates and duration time independently and then normalized the time scale by the duration (in days), making the maximum duration time equal to 1. The normalized time evolution pat terns show that more earthquakes occurred in the early stage for all three sequences (Figure 4b). After summing the three time<sup>‐</sup>normalized sequences, we obtained a robust time evolution measurement showing that Cluster n is an aftershock<sup>‐</sup>like sequence in which most earthquakes occur early in the sequence and there is clear decay with time, supporting the idea that Cluster n represents late aftershocks of the Hebgen Lake earthquake.

Overall, we interpret Cluster n as part of the long<sup>‐</sup>lived aftershock sequence of the 1959 $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { w } } 7 . 2$ Hebgen Lake earthquake, similar to our interpretation of the 2014<sup>–</sup>2017 seismicity near Challis, ID, as primarily after shocks of the 1983 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 6.9 Borah Peak earthquake (Pang et al., 2018), with some influence of fluid diffusion. Aftershock sequences in low<sup>‐</sup>strain<sup>‐</sup>rate regions can last hundreds of years (Castro et al., 2010; Ebel et al., 2000; Pang et al., 2018; Stein & Liu, 2009; Toda & Stein, 2018), and there is evidence that aftershock sequences in extensional tectonic settings are especially long (Valerio et al., 2017). The aftershock duration time in years, $t _ { \mathbf { a } } ,$ can be estimated by applying $t _ { \mathbf { a } } = 3 1 4 / \nu$ from Stein and Liu (2009), where v is the loading rate in millimeters per year. Considering the regional extensional strain rate determined from GPS measurements (Payne et al., 2012), we estimate a loading rate of \~3 mm/year, corresponding to an aftershock duration of \~100 years for the 1959 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake earthquake.

## 5.2. The Southern Portion of the 2017–2018 Maple Creek Sequence

In contrast to the northern portion, the southern portion of the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek sequence (Cluster s) consists mainly of EW<sup>‐</sup>striking planar structures. Many of these structures dip to the north, opposite to the dip of the Maple Creek section of the Red Canyon fault. These earthquakes are also shallower than those in the northern sequence, making them closer to the hypothesized $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } .$ filled body at shallow depths (Husen et al., 2004). In addition, the southern epicenters are adjacent to the boundary of the 2.1<sup>‐</sup>Ma caldera (Figure 2), and the south<sup>‐</sup>to<sup>‐</sup>north migration pattern for Clusters s<sup>‐</sup>II, s<sup>‐</sup>III, s<sup>‐</sup>IV, and s<sup>‐</sup>V is similar to the migration pattern of the three previous major Yellowstone swarms, all of which have been associated with magmatic fluids (Massin et al., 2013).

We applied the same temporal evolution analysis to these southern events as we did for the northern events. We divided the earthquakes into three subsequences based on their occurrence time (Figure 4a) and equal ized the time scales by dividing by the duration times. The normalized time evolution pattern is significantly different from the northern events. Instead of occurring early in the sequences, the earthquake rates reach a maximum in the middle or later portion of the normalized duration (Figure 4c). The average daily seismicity rate in the sequences gradually increases to a maximum and then slowly decays to the background level indi cating that the sequences are more swarm like than aftershock like. Cluster s<sup>‐</sup>I initiated during Cluster n and the very small sequence (blue in Figures 4 and S7) in late October 2017 looks like a precursor to Cluster s<sup>‐</sup>II. Combined with the south<sup>‐</sup>to<sup>‐</sup>north migration feature and the stable in situ $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio, the clusters in the south are likely a swarm induced by fluids or gases, perhaps with some stress interaction from the aftershock<sup>‐</sup>like events in Cluster n.

## 6. Conclusions

The 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek earthquake sequence was the fourth major burst of seismicity in the Yellowstone region since it was first instrumented with seismometers in 1972. It can be divided into two dis tinct subsequences: an aftershock<sup>‐</sup>like sequence for the northern portion and a swarm<sup>‐</sup>like sequence for the southern portion. Overall, we interpret the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek earthquake sequence as a tectonic after shock sequence of the 1959 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake earthquake that was influenced by magmatic fluids asso ciated with the nearby Yellowstone volcanic system.

We used waveform<sup>‐</sup>derived differential travel times to generate high<sup>‐</sup>precision relative relocations and estimate $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios in the near<sup>‐</sup>source region. We observed several planar structures and low $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios with significant spatial variations. The low $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratios preclude significant amounts of partial melt in the source region and instead indicate the presence of cracks filled with either $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ gas or aqueous solutions $( \mathrm { e . g . , C O _ { 2 } / H _ { 2 } O }$ mixture). In the northern portion of the sequence, the planes tended to dip steeply and strike either NW<sup>‐</sup>SE or NE<sup>–</sup>SW, similar to the two nodal planes of the largest event in the sequence $( \mathbf { M } _ { \mathbf { w } }$ 4.4 on 16 June 2017). This geometry is consistent with the regional stress field and the faulting pattern in the nearby 1959 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake rupture zone. The $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ reduction in this region from 1.66 in the southeast to 1.40 in the northwest may be a manifestation of increased fault porosity/density as one approaches the still<sup>‐</sup> damaged 1959 $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake rupture zone. In the southern portion of the sequence the planar structures had shallower dips and nearly EW strikes, consistent with Quaternary faults located southeast of the Hebgen Lake rupture zone, closer to the 0.63<sup>‐</sup>Ma caldera boundary. The $V _ { P } / V _ { S }$ ratio of $1 . 5 0 5 \pm 0 . 0 0 2$ for this region is lower than the north portion of the sequence, but we did not observe significant variations within the southern portion.

We suggest that the southern portion of the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek earthquake sequence is a swarm induced by migrating fluids associated with the Yellowstone volcanic system, similar in nature to the prolific 1985 Yellowstone swarm. This interpretation is supported by the south<sup>‐</sup>to<sup>‐</sup>north space<sup>–</sup>time migration of events, the EW orientation of planar structures in the seismicity, the proximity of the 2.1<sup>‐</sup>Ma caldera bound ary, and the relative proximity of the upper crustal magma reservoir beneath the 0.63<sup>‐</sup>Ma caldera boundary. We interpret the northern portion of the 2017<sup>–</sup>2018 Maple Creek earthquake sequence as primarily late aftershocks of the 1959 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake earthquake but with some influence of fluid diffusion (Figures S5 and S6) as noted by Shelly and Hardebeck (2019). These earthquakes are deeper and closer to the Hebgen Lake rupture zone and form primarily NW<sup>‐</sup>SE planar structures, similar to the Hebgen Lake fault system. Their magnitude<sup>‐</sup>time distribution is more front loaded and Omori like compared to the clas sical swarm<sup>‐</sup>like distribution observed for the southern sequence. The northern sequence of earthquakes also occurs in a region of increased positive Coulomb failure stress created by the 1959 ${ { \mathbf { M } } _ { \mathrm { w } } }$ 7.2 Hebgen Lake earthquake (Figure S3).
