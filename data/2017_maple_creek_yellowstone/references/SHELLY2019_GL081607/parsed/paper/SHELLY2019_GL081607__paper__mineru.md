# Illuminating Faulting Complexity of the 2017 Yellowstone Maple Creek Earthquake Swarm

David R. Shelly<sup>1</sup> and Jeanne L. Hardebeck<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Geologic Hazards Science Center, U.S. Geological Survey, Golden, CO, USA, <sup>2</sup>Earthquake Science Center, U.S. Geological Survey, Menlo Park, CA, USA

Abstract The 2017 Maple Creek earthquake swarm was one of the most prolific swarms to occur in the Yellowstone region in the past few decades, with nearly 2,500 routinely detected earthquakes up to $M _ { w }$ 4.4 between June and September 2017. To gain insight into the mechanics and underlying mechanisms of this swarm, we enhanced the seismic catalog, detecting and precisely locating nearly 16,000 earthquakes, while estimating magnitudes for more than 30,000 events. We further utilized the cross<sup>‐</sup>correlation measurements derived from this processing to determine relative polarities for event pairs and to estimate focal mechanisms for the relocated population of events. The results reveal a complex network of faults activated progressively during the swarm, which may reflect diffusion of aqueous fluid pressure through these structures. Fluid<sup>‐</sup>driven earthquake sequences may naturally generate more complex faulting geometries compared to earthquake sequences dominated by stress transfer.

Plain Language Summary We examine an earthquake swarm that occurred in the Yellowstone region from June to September 2017. To gain insight into the swarm, we enhanced the routine seismic catalog to include many more very small earthquakes and to precisely locate all events. In total, we located nearly 16,000 earthquakes and estimated magnitudes for more than 30,000 events. We also used a recently developed technique to estimate the orientation of slip for these earthquakes. This analysis reveals a complex spatial and temporal evolution of faulting in the earthquake swarm, which we hypothesize is related to changes in fluid (water) pressure driving the swarm.

## 1. Introduction

Earthquake swarms, sequences of seismicity deviating from typical main shock<sup>‐</sup>aftershock patterns, have been a long<sup>‐</sup>running topic of investigation (e.g., Mogi, 1963). A recurrent question is the physical mechanism(s) by which earthquake swarms sustain (or increase) their activity rates, differentiating them from typical main shock<sup>‐</sup>aftershock sequences that are characterized by an obvious main shock followed by a decaying seismicity sequence. The most commonly proposed mechanisms for swarm behavior are triggering by aseismic slip and/or transient increases in fluid pressure (e.g., Chen et al., 2012; Vidale & Shearer, 2006). For most swarms, the evidence for either mechanism is largely circumstantial, relying primarily on infer ence from spatial<sup>–</sup>temporal patterns of seismicity.

Swarms are particularly common in volcanic and hydrothermal regions, such as Iceland (e.g., Klein et al., 1977), Western Bohemia (e.g., Fischer et al., 2014), and Japan (e.g., Yukutake et al., 2011). The Yellowstone Caldera and surrounding volcanic field, United States, is another notable region, hosting major swarms in 1985 (Waite & Smith, 2002), 2008<sup>–</sup>2009 (Farrell et al., 2010), and 2010 (Shelly et al., 2013; Figure 1) and frequent smaller swarms. Despite long periods of eruptive quiescence (the last caldera<sup>‐</sup>forming eruption occurred \~631 ka (Matthews et al., 2015), and the last magmatic eruption \~70 ka (Christiansen, 2001)), Yellowstone Caldera and surroundings remain dynamic, with intense hydrothermal activity, ground deformation, and seismicity (Smith et al., 2009) underlain by a large magma body (Farrell et al., 2014).

Here we examine the 2017 <sup>“</sup>Maple Creek<sup>”</sup> earthquake swarm (Pang et al., 2017), which began in June 2017. Its main three<sup>‐</sup>month phase of activity included nearly 2,500 routinely cataloged events, with magnitudes ranging up to $M _ { w }$ 4.4, recorded on a network of 27 seismic stations within 70 km (Figure 1). This swarm was the largest to occur since significant seismic network upgrades in 2013, when multiple sites were upgraded with three<sup>‐</sup>component broadband sensors. Thus, this sequence was probably the best<sup>‐</sup>recorded large swarm in Yellowstone to date.

## 2. Methods

To investigate the 2017 Maple Creek swarm, we worked to enhance the seismic catalog to reveal the detailed spatial<sup>–</sup>temporal evolution of seismicity. To achieve this, we leveraged continuous seismic data along with the routinely produced earthquake catalog and associated phase picks from University of Utah Seismograph Stations (University of Utah, 1984). We then performed a series of analyses, including waveform<sup>‐</sup>based earthquake detection, relocation, and (for newly detected events) magnitude estimation. For these analyses, we followed the procedures as described in Shelly, Ellsworth, et al. (2016) and briefly summarized below. Finally, we used the signed cross<sup>‐</sup>correlation measurements derived from this proces sing to group events with similar patterns of P and S phase polarities across the network and to determine their focal mechanisms, following the technique presented by Shelly, Hardebeck, et al. (2016).

We used 2289 routinely cataloged events from 11 June to 6 September 2017 as waveform templates. Templates were formed on all velocity channels of any station with a catalog phase pick, windowed starting 0.5 s prior to the phase pick. Templates lengths were 2.5 s (for P) or 4.0 s (for S), with the P template truncated if necessary to avoid overlapping with the S template. Data were sampled at 100 Hz. In cases where no S wave pick was available, we windowed the S template based on the catalog origin time, the P wave arrival time, and a P/S velocity ratio of 1.5 (deliberately low to avoid missing the S wave onset). Template waveforms were scanned through the continuous data at increments of 0.01 s (one sample), with all waveforms filtered 2<sup>–</sup>15 Hz. Event detections were declared and differential times were measured and weighted as described in Shelly, Ellsworth, et al. (2016), considering the absolute value correlation coefficients, the median absolute deviation of the correlations for the day, and the heights of nearby correlation peaks. All tem plate events were scanned through each day of continuous data from 11 June to 14 September.

This processing produced a total of34.1 million correlation<sup>‐</sup>derived differential times, which were then input into the hypoDD double<sup>‐</sup>difference relocation algorithm (Waldhauser & Ellsworth, 2000) along with 1.3 mil lion differential times derived from catalog phase picks. Catalog data were weighted most strongly early in the inversion, while correlation data controlled later iterations. In total, 15,912 earthquakes retained at leas 50 P and 50 S correlation observations through the inversion and can be considered well located.

To determine magnitudes of newly detected events, we followed a procedure similar to that described by Shelly, Ellsworth, et al. (2016). Magnitudes for events in the catalog were retained, with magnitudes for newly detected events estimated based on amplitude ratios measured with cataloged events. We followed the formulation:

$$
M _ {\mathrm{new}} = M _ {\mathrm{template}} + c \log_ {1 0} (\alpha)
$$

where $M _ { \mathrm { n e w } }$ is the magnitude of the newly detected event, $M _ { \mathrm { t e m p l a t e } }$ is the magnitude of the catalog event used as a template, α is the measured amplitude ratio, and c is a constant describing the amplitudemagnitude scaling.

For template magnitudes, we used $M _ { c }$ (<sup>“</sup>coda<sup>”</sup> or duration) magnitudes reported by University of Utah Seismograph Stations<sup>—</sup> ${ \mathbf { \nabla } } \cdot M _ { c } \mathbf { \nabla }$ are the only magnitudes reported for the smaller catalog earthquakes; coda mag nitudes are usually also reported for the larger events for which $M _ { L }$ (local or Richter magnitude) or $M _ { w }$ (moment magnitude) are preferred. Rather than mixing magnitude types, we relied on $M _ { c }$ for all events.

We measured the relative amplitude α between the template and corresponding detected waveforms for each data channel and phase for which the correlation value exceeded 7 times the median absolute deviation for that channel for the day. As with other processing steps, relative amplitudes were measured using data band pass filtered 2<sup>–</sup>15 Hz. The optimal scaling between the waveforms was determined using a principal component fit to the entire template window (2.5 s for P or 4.0 s for S). This procedure avoids instability inherent in peak amplitude measurements, and the principal component fit preserves reciprocity (i.e., the amplitude ratio is simply inversed if template and newly detected events are exchanged). For each detected event of each template, we computed the median of the measured amplitude ratio values from all data channels and phases. Finally, we used the median magnitude estimate derived from all template events detecting a given event in the continuous data.

The appropriate value for c was determined empirically for consistency with the scaling observed in the catalog magnitudes. Figure S1 shows the distribution of relative amplitude measurements for pairs of catalog events. The slope in this case is ${ \sim } 1 . 0 ,$ , the same as would be expected for $M _ { L }$ in this magnitude range<sup>—</sup>that is, both $M _ { c }$ and $M _ { L }$ scale directly with the logarithm of the seismic moment, in contrast to moment magni tude $( M _ { w } ;$ Deichmann, 2017; Hanks & Boore, 1984; Shelly, Ellsworth, et al. (2016)), which is defined to scale as 2/3 times the log of the seismic moment (Hanks & Kanamori, 1979). Interestingly, this scaling is distinct from the value of \~0.8 found for the Northern California Seismic Network duration magnitudes $( M _ { d } )$ in Long Valley Caldera, California, probably because different formulas and procedures are employed (Arabasz et al., 2006; Eaton, 1992). Because less data are needed to constrain event magnitude than event location, we use less stringent criteria of retaining at least 15 P and 30 S observations through the inversion, resulting in a magnitude catalog of 30,411 events.

Finally, we used the 34.1 million signed correlation measurements (described above) to group events with similar patterns of polarities across the network, and to estimate the associated focal mechanisms, following the procedure described by Shelly, Hardebeck, et al. (2016). This technique relies on the premise that wave forms for two earthquakes on a given data channel can be either similar or antisimilar, as indicated by the sign of the correlation coefficient with peak absolute value. This sign is then a proxy for the relative polarity between the two events. The peak correlation coefficient is expected to be positive for two events with the same polarity and negative if they have opposite polarity (Ide et al., 2007). We use the difference in amplitude between the largest and second<sup>‐</sup>largest peaks as a weighting parameter reflecting relative confidence in each measurement. We grouped events using hierarchical clustering based on polarities for both P and S on al data channels. As in Shelly, Hardebeck, et al. (2016), we specified division into 100 total clusters, with the majority of events concentrated in just a few populous clusters. Once clustered, a double<sup>‐</sup>couple<sup>‐</sup>constrained composite focal mechanism was estimated for each cluster using correlation<sup>‐</sup>derived polarities for vertical channel P waves only, using a version of HASH (Hardebeck & Shearer, 2002) modified to accept weighted polarities. The solutions examined here follow the procedure described by Shelly, Hardebeck, et al. (2016) as the <sup>“</sup>correlation composite<sup>”</sup> solution, where catalog phase data are used only to resolve the polarity sign ambiguity inherent in cross<sup>‐</sup>correlation measurements. During the course of this analysis, the vertical channel of station YMC was found to be reversed in polarity compared to available metadata; the solutions presented here correct for that reversal.

## 3. Results

Figure 2 shows the spatial and temporal structure of the swarm as seen in the newly enhanced earthquake catalog. This catalog reveals numerous features obscured in the routine catalog locations, including a com plex network of faults progressively activated during the swarm and rapid migration on these structures. The most prominent seismicity features strike ENE; however, several seismicity features also strike toward the NW.

Correlation<sup>‐</sup>based event clustering and associated focal mechanisms enhance the distinction between these dual fault orientations. Figure 3 shows the locations of clustered events along with the corresponding mechanism estimated for each cluster, highlighting the eight most populous clusters. The vast majority of events (95% of total located events) belong to one of these top eight clusters, while 81% belong to clusters 1<sup>–</sup>3 (Figure 3d). Clusters 1, 2, 5, and 7 appear to reflect faulting with an ENE strike, with right<sup>‐</sup>lateral strike<sup>‐</sup>slip motion and/or normal faulting. In contrast, clusters 3 and 4 seem to strike toward the NW, with left<sup>‐</sup>lateral strike slip and/or normal faulting. Clusters 6 and 8 are more difficult to confidently categorize. Clusters 3 and 4 may reflect faulting conjugate to that indicated in clusters 1, 2, 5, and 7. Mechanism solutions with associated polarities for each event cluster are shown in Figure S2. Movie S3 in the supporting information shows the evolution of activity in each cluster over the course of the swarm, including times when differing<sup>‐</sup>mechanism events (e.g., clusters 2 and 3) are concurrently active in the same area.

The correlation<sup>‐</sup>based focal mechanism technique groups events based on similar patterns of polarities across the network and thus works best when the spatial extent of events is small compared to the network aperture. Although this criterion is mostly true in this case, seismic station YMC is located almost directly above the swarm (Figure 1). Because of its proximity, polarities at this station may be strongly affected by small changes in hypocenter, even without changes in mechanism. We experimented with removing this station for purposes of focal mechanism grouping, but this appeared to degrade the ability to distinguish between different faulting orientations. The inclusion of this station may cause splitting of events with very similar mechanism into different clusters based on their locations, but we judged this to be an acceptable compromise in this case.

Movies S1 and S2 in the supporting information highlight the complexity of seismicity during the swarm. The swarm initiated on 12 June 2017 at 16:46 UTC, with two $M _ { L } \sim 3$ events within the first 10 s, at a depth approaching 10 km below mean surface elevation (mean surface is \~2 km above sea level in this area). Following this initiation, seismicity expanded in spatial extent, mostly shallower and toward the ENE, before the largest event, with $M _ { w } = 4 . 4$ , occurred on 16 June. The seismicity rate increased following this event, activating an apparently conjugate NW striking fault (mechanism cluster #3<sup>—</sup>see Figure 3) as wel as the primary ENE striking fault zone. In the days and weeks following, seismicity continued to expand, migrating laterally along the main ENE striking fault zone as well as activating a complex sequence of struc tures both shallower and deeper. Notably, swarm activity was reinvigorated in early August, with nearly simultaneous activation of shallower (\~8<sup>‐</sup>km depth) faults toward the WNW and deeper activity almost directly below the primary swarm area (see Movie S2 in the supporting information). The reason for this near<sup>‐</sup>simultaneous activation is unclear. A M 3.1 event in the shallower zone (at 13:16 on 1 August) might have triggered activity in the deeper zone, or a fluid pressure transient might have affected both shallow and deeper zones in short succession. Nevertheless, we cannot rule out random coincidence. During this epi sode, the shallower zone migrated laterally back toward the primary swarm zone, apparently activating a series of en echelon faults in the process (Figure 3c). In contrast, the deeper zone migrated downward to \~11<sup>‐</sup>km depth, away from the primary zone, along a NW striking fault likely conjugate to the dominant ENE orientation (mechanism cluster #4). Sporadic activity continued through late August and early September, including cluster #7 that seemed to activate an ENE striking fault at the downdip edge of the swarm. Overall, late<sup>‐</sup>stage seismicity mostly activated areas at the fringe of previously active zones (Figure 2 and Movies S1 and S2 in the supporting information).

The superset catalog of event magnitudes (see section 2), containing more than 30,000 events, provides an opportunity to examine the frequency<sup>‐</sup>magnitude distribution and its evolution through the course of the swarm. To calculate a Gutenberg<sup>‐</sup>Richter b value, we used a cutoff magnitude of −0.5, somewhat more con servative than the suggestion to use a magnitude 0.2 units higher than the maximum frequency magnitude bin (Wiemer & Wyss, 2000; Woessner & Wiemer, 2005). This provided nearly 10,000 total events above the cutoff magnitude for the swarm, with an overall b value of 0.74 as determined by maximum likelihood (Tint & Mulargia, 1987; Figure S3). However, as noted in section 2, we are using an $M _ { L } .$ <sup>‐</sup>like magnitude scale at small magnitudes, with magnitude scaling as \~1.0\*log moment, rather than ⅔\*log (moment) as with $M _ { w }$ (Deichmann, 2017; Hanks & Kanamori, 1979). Therefore, if using an $M _ { w }$ scale, we would expect the magni tudes to be substantially more compressed, producing an \~50% higher b value of \~1.1. In either scenario, the resultant b value falls within the range commonly observed for tectonic seismicity, and within the range of \~0.6 to \~1.5 previously reported for Yellowstone seismicity (Farrell et al., 2009). The b value varies somewhat over the course of the swarm<sup>—</sup>using a moving window of 1,000 events above the cutoff magnitude, b values vary from a high of 0.86 in late June to a low of 0.59 in early August, as shown in Figure S3c. Although there is a reported association between high b values and fluids in the literature (e.g., Wiemer & McNutt, 1997), in detail this relationship is likely much more complex, perhaps influenced by the magnitude of fluid pressure change (Bachmann et al., 2012) and fault size distributions that vary according to the degree of fluid confinement (Shelly, Ellsworth, et al., 2016). Studies have also proposed that the b value varies inversely with stress levels (Goebel et al., 2013; Schorlemmer et al., 2005). In this context, relatively high b values in late June could reflect stress release from the 16 June $M _ { w }$ 4.4 earthquake; however, if so, continuing high seismicity rates might require a concurrent drop in fault strength.

## 4. Discussion and Conclusions

The geometric complexity of seismicity in this swarm is apparent. In particular, correlation<sup>‐</sup>based focal mechanism analysis reveals coincident (in space and time) activation of faulting at apparently conjugate orientations (Figure 3c). At the smaller scales, these faulting styles would be indistinguishable without this focal mechanism analysis, even with precise event locations.

Faulting complexity seems to be a recurrent feature of fluid<sup>‐</sup>induced seismicity (Yamashita & Tsutsumi, 2018), including seismicity stemming from fluid injection (e.g., Deichmann et al., 2014) and seismicity inferred to result from fluid pressure effects in hydrothermal regions, such as Hakone Caldera, Japan (Yukutake et al., 2011), and Western Bohemia, Czech Republic (e.g., Fischer et al., 2014). These swarms often activate multiple fault structures of differing orientations and focal mechanisms (Cox, 2016). Although main shock<sup>‐</sup>aftershock sequences sometimes also exhibit high levels of complexity (e.g., Cesca et al., 2017), such complexity is perhaps rare in comparison to that found in fluid<sup>‐</sup>induced swarms. In the case of fluid<sup>‐</sup>induced swarms, complex faulting may be activated by fluid diffusion through crust with simi larly complex permeability structure. Complexity may also arise from fault mesh structures that can accom modate hybrid shear<sup>‐</sup>dilational failure (Hill, 1977; Sibson, 1996). Fault mesh structures are a common feature of the geologic record in areas of mineral deposition inferred to have resulted from repeated, episodic hydrothermal<sup>‐</sup>fluid<sup>‐</sup>induced earthquake swarms (Cox, 2016; Sibson, 1996).

Although the swarm exhibits pronounced migration with time, the expansion is not well described by a simple square<sup>‐</sup>root of time diffusive pattern (e.g., Shapiro et al., 1997; Figure S4). However, this may not be surprising, and it does not invalidate a hypothesis of fluid triggering, as we expect the underlying assumption of homogeneous 3<sup>‐</sup>D diffusion to be grossly violated. As with other swarms (e.g., Hainzl et al., 2012; Shelly et al., 2013; Shelly, Ellsworth, et al., 2016), this swarm exhibited both upward and downward migration (Figure S4). Downward migration may seem counterintuitive for a fluid<sup>‐</sup>driven earthquake swarm, yet it is consistent with our expectations of large variations in fluid pressure. Fluid pressure should be approximately lithostatic in the ductile regime, where the rock matrix does not sup port the long<sup>‐</sup>term lithostatic load, while fluid pressure may be approximately hydrostatic in the brittle regime (Fournier, 1999). At depths of \~8<sup>–</sup>11 km seen in this swarm, this creates pressure differentials potentially much larger than local pressure gradients. Because of heterogeneous and dynamic permeabil ity, these pressure differentials can result in locally downward pressure diffusion. For example, if the mid point of a vertically extensive, hydrostatically pressured fault zone becomes hydraulically connected to a deeper lithostatic pressure regime, this pressure will diffuse both upward and downward within the fault zone, as near<sup>‐</sup>lithostatic pressure would exceed the initial pressure at even the lower limit of this hypothe tical fault. We also note that later episodes of activity, beyond the three<sup>‐</sup>month period examined here, occurred in close proximity to the main swarm zone, including a sizable episode in February 2018. This continued activity may reflect prolonged elevated fluid pressures in the swarm zone, which contin ued to diffuse long after the most active phase of the swarm.

In contrast to the swarm evolution being controlled by aqueous fluid pressure diffusion, an alternative scenario of triggering dominated by aseismic slip is more difficult to construct. Seismicity in this swarm is not confined to small asperities within an otherwise creeping fault; rather, assuming reasonable stress drops, the area of seismic slip fully covers large areas of the fault zone. The faults could slip in both seismic and aseismic slip; however, invoking aseismic slip as the dominant triggering mechanism would seem to require a carefully orchestrated geometry of aseismic slip on a series of faults in close proximity to those slipping seismi cally, such that stress would be progressively transferred from the aseismically slipping faults to the seismic faults. Given the lack of supporting evidence for this kind of arrangement, we consider such a scenario unlikely.

The 2017 swarm also occurred just east of the 1959 $M _ { w } \ : 7 . 3$ Hebgen Lake earthquake. Long<sup>‐</sup>lived aftershock sequences have been proposed to occur in some locations of low<sup>‐</sup>strain accumulation (Stein & Liu, 2009), and continuing stress relaxation from the 1959 earthquake (Chang et al., 2013; Nishimura & Thatcher, 2003; Reilinger, 1986) might plausibly enhance stress accumulation and seismicity in this area. However, the swarm also occurred at the intersection of two corridors of elevated seismicity: one extending NW from Yellowstone Caldera that includes the 1985 and 2010 swarms and another extending WNW from Sour Creek Dome in the NE part of the caldera (Figure 1). These zones may delineate pathways of aqueous fluid transport from the caldera, where aqueous<sup>‐</sup>rich fluids are probably exsolved from crystallizing pockets of magma (Fournier, 1999; Savage et al., 1993; Shelly et al., 2013; Waite & Smith, 2002; Wicks et al., 2006). As discussed above, the spatial and temporal patterns of the swarm strongly suggest fluid<sup>‐</sup>pressure trigger ing, lending support to the earlier hypothesis that these seismicity zones may reflect fluid pathways, with or without influence from the 1959 earthquake.

Swarms such as this likely play an important role in producing concentrated mineral deposits (e.g., Cox, 2016), perhaps aided by flash vaporization resulting from sudden pressure drops as slip on nonplanar faults increases pore space (e.g., Weatherley & Henley, 2013). If so, vaporization might be an important process driving continued swarm activity<sup>—</sup>for example, if $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ comes out of solution during faulting, it may gener ate an overall increase in pressure, because $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ does not return to solution as rapidly as it is released (e.g., Crews & Cooper, 2014; Weinlich, 2014)<sup>—</sup>the same effect as the net pressure increase caused by shaking a soda bottle. Such an effect would tend to prolong swarm activity once it begins. A low ratio of P/S velocities $( V _ { p } / V _ { s }$ ratio) in the general region above the swarm was interpreted as a gas (likely $\mathbf { C O } _ { 2 } )$ reservoir (Husen et al., 2004), although a subsequent study questioned the viability of such a reservoir, based on relatively low surface emissions of $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ at springs in the area (Evans et al., 2006).

Fluids likely play a major role in cycles of ground deformation and seismicity in Yellowstone<sup>—</sup>indeed, past major swarms (including 1985 and 2010 swarms) accompanied a transition from uplift to subsidence of the caldera, which might be explained by increased permeability and associated lateral aqueous fluid diffusion related to swarm activity on the caldera perimeter (Shelly et al., 2013; Waite & Smith, 2002). The 2017 swarm, however, did not obviously influence deformation patterns.
