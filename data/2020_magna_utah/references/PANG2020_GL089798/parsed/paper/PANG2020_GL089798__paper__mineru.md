# Seismic Analysis of the 2020 Magna, Utah, Earthquake Sequence: Evidence for a Listric Wasatch Fault

Guanning Pang<sup>1</sup> , Keith D. Koper<sup>1</sup> , Maria Mesimeri<sup>1</sup> , Kristine L. Pankow<sup>1</sup>, Ben Baker<sup>1</sup> , Jamie Farrell<sup>1</sup> , James Holt<sup>1</sup> , J. <sup>1</sup>Department of Geology and Geophysics, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, UT, USA, <sup>2</sup>Geologic Hazards Science Center, U.S. Geological Survey, Golden, CO, USA

Abstract The 18 March 2020 M<sub>w</sub> 5.7 Magna earthquake near Salt Lake City, Utah, offers a rare glimpse into the subsurface geometry of the Wasatch fault system—one of the world's longest active normal faults and a major source of seismic hazard in northern Utah. We analyze the Magna earthquake sequence and resolve oblique‐normal slip on a shallow (30–35°) west‐dipping fault at \~9‐ to 12‐km depth. Combined with near‐surface geological observations of steep dip (\~70°), our results support a curved, or listric, fault shape. High‐precision aftershock locations show the activation of multiple, low‐angle (<30–35°) structures, indicating the existence of a complicated fault system. Our observations constrain the deep structure of the Wasatch fault system and suggest that ground shaking in the Salt Lake City region in future Wasatch fault earthquakes may be higher than previously estimated.

Plain Language Summary On 18 March 2020, a moment magnitude (M<sub>w</sub>) 5.7 earthquake occurred beneath Magna, Utah, a suburb of Salt Lake City. It was the largest earthquake in the Wasatch fault system in historical times, and shaking was felt throughout northern Utah. The mainshock and its aftershocks were located in the middle of a dense seismic network, generating a rare and valuable data set of strong ground accelerations from normal‐faulting earthquakes. We analyzed the first \~6 weeks of seismic data following the mainshock and found that the aftershocks formed a shallow‐dipping planar structure at depths of 9–12 km, implying that this segment of the Wasatch fault zone has a curved shape. At the surface, it dips steeply to the west at \~70°, but as the depth increases, the dip becomes progressively more shallow. Simulations of ground shaking from large earthquakes previously assumed that this fault segment was planar and steeply dipping (\~50°) throughout the upper crust. Our observations suggest that the rupture area of future large earthquakes will be closer to the surface than previously thought, which would cause increased ground shaking in the Salt Lake City metropolitan area with its \~1.2 million residents.

## 1. Introduction

The Wasatch fault zone (WFZ) is the master, range‐bounding normal fault system in northern Utah. It divides the stable Colorado Plateau and southern Rocky Mountain physiographic provinces to the east from the actively extending Basin and Range province to the west (Wannamaker et al., 2001). The WFZ accommodates 2–3 mm/yr of nearly east–west extension (Chang et al., 2006; Kreemer et al., 2010) along 10 major structural segments (Machette et al., 1991) that together span \~350 km (Figure 1). Paleoseismological obser vations indicate that 24 surface‐rupturing earthquakes—thought to be magnitude 6.75 or larger—have occurred in the past 7,000 years on the five central segments of the WFZ (DuRoss et al., 2016), making the WFZ the largest contributor to seismic hazard in the region (Petersen et al., 2019). Because most of Utah's 3.2 million residents are concentrated in an urban corridor that spans the WFZ and related faults, the regio nal seismic risk is severe. The highest risk occurs for the Salt Lake City metropolitan area, an urban core of 1.2 million people that lies in a sedimentary basin directly above the west‐dipping Wasatch fault. An M7 earthquake occurring on the Salt Lake City segment (SLCS) of the WFZ is projected to cause 2,000–2,500 fatalities and a short‐term economic loss over \$33 billion (Pankow et al., 2015).

One model, based largely on focal mechanisms determined from past normal faulting continental earthquakes, suggests that normal faults are planar and dip $5 0 ^ { \circ } \pm 1 5 ^ { \circ }$ (Collettini & Sibson, 2001; Doser & Smith, 1989). Chang et al. (2006) show that a steeply dipping (55–70°) planar structure is also consistent with geodetic data collected along the Wasatch front. Seismic hazard modeling for northern Utah has largely adopted this structure for the WFZ, employing a dip of $5 0 ^ { \circ } ~ \pm ~ 1 5 ^ { \circ }$ (Petersen et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2016). Ground motion modeling (Moschetti et al., 2017; Roten et al., 2011) and ShakeMap scenarios (Pankow et al., 2015) of hypothetical M7 earthquakes on the SLCS also use a dip of \~50°. An alternative model, based on regional seismic reflection profiles (Smith & Bruhn, 1984; Velasco et al., 2010) and geodetic observations (Bennett et al., 2007; Savage et al., 1992; Velasco et al., 2010), proposes that the Wasatch fault is listric with dips ${ < } 3 0 ^ { \circ }$ in the deeper sections of the fault zone where the fault is assumed to be continuously slipping. A listric structure is also consistent with the potential geometry of the antithetic West Valley fault zone, which is likely structurally linked to the Wasatch fault based on the timing of ruptures along both faults (DuRoss & Hylland, 2015). A listric Wasatch fault would increase ground motion predictions for future scenario earthquakes by increasing the downdip width of the rupture zone and decreasing its average depth.

On 18 March 2020, an $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ 5.7 oblique normal‐faulting earthquake occurred near Magna, Utah, about 10 km west of downtown Salt Lake City (epicenter: 40.751°N, 112.078°W) and 11.9 km beneath sea level (13.2 km beneath the surface) (Figure 1). The epicenter was \~10–12 km west of the surface trace of the SLCS. There were no serious injuries or fatalities, but initial damage estimates exceed \$150 million. It was the largest earthquake to occur in the WFZ in historical times, and it occurred within the densest part of the Utah Regional Seismic Network (Pankow et al., 2020). The mainshock was recorded by \~120 seismograph stations within 150 km. Here we report on characteristics of the Magna earthquake sequence that are relevant to seis mic hazard. We estimate the area of the mainshock rupture and interpret it in the context of an SLCS strain budget, and we perform a time‐dependent b value analysis to assess the likelihood that a larger event is yet to occur as part of the Magna sequence (Gulia & Wiemer, 2019). We also combine moment tensor inversion with aftershock detection and high‐precision relocation to constrain the SLCS fault architecture beneath the Salt Lake City metropolitan area.

## 2. Seismic Data and Initial Earthquake Locations

At the time of the mainshock, the University of Utah Seismograph Stations (UUSS) operated a combined urban‐regional seismic network in the Utah region composed of 203 seismograph stations, including 40 broadband, 97 strong‐motion, and 66 short‐period sensors (Figures S1 and S2). A total of 52 UUSS accelerometers located within 50 km of the mainshock epicenter recorded data (Figure 2). The mainshock was also recorded by eight UU strong‐motion NetQuakes accelerometers and nine National Strong Motion Project strong‐motion instruments, three of which were installed inside man‐made structures, at distances of 10– 100 km from the mainshock epicenter. The combined strong‐motion data set for the mainshock is unprecedented for a normal‐faulting earthquake and validates the decades‐long commitment to seismic monitoring in northern Utah by UUSS and the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) (Pankow et al., 2020). In the days following the mainshock, UUSS personnel installed three strong‐motion stations and two combined broadband/strong‐motion stations—one colocated with an existing strong‐motion station—and added a broadband sensor at another existing strong‐motion station. All five temporary installations were telemetered, and all were within 45 km of the epicenter (Figure S3). An additional 180 5‐Hz, three‐component, Magseis Fairfield nodal geophones were deployed around the epicentral region in the week following the mainshock (Figure S3), though none of the nodal data were used in this study. Using the telemetered seismic data, as of 30 April 2020, UUSS analysts had finalized locations for 2,103 earthquakes in the Magna sequence (Figure 1), ofwhich 2,077 were assigned at least one magnitude type (Table S2). No foreshocks were detected (see Text S1 for details on routine UUSS processing of seismic network data).

## 3. Analysis and Results

## 3.1. Regional Moment Tensor Inversion

We inverted regional‐distance broadband waveforms with a time‐domain, least‐squares procedure for full moment tensors (Minson & Dreger, 2008; Whidden & Pankow, 2012). Green's functions were calculated using frequency‐wavenumber integration (Saikia, 1994) with the western United States (WUS) velocity model (Herrmann et al., 2011). We instrument‐corrected the observed three‐component broadband seismo grams to displacement, rotated horizontal components into radial and transverse, downsampled the traces to $^ { 1 \mathrm { H z } , }$ and applied a band‐pass filter at 10–20 s. To be used in the modeling, we required seismograms to have appropriate surface wave polarization and a signal‐to‐noise ratio ≥3. We manually time shifted the synthetic waveforms to best match the data to mitigate inaccuracies in the 1D velocity model. The max imum shift allowed was 5 s or half of the shortest period used. For depth determination, we used a combina tion of maximum variance reduction (VR) and the minimum residual divided by the percent double couple (res/Pdc). Typically, the highest VR and the lowest res/Pdc occur at similar depths. In order to accept a solu tion as valid, we required a minimum VR of \~70% and good azimuthal station coverage.

For the mainshock, we found an oblique‐normal mechanism with a moment magnitude of $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ 5.54 and a centroid depth of 9 km below the surface (Figure S4). This depth is \~4 km shallower than the hypocenter determined with travel times and may indicate upward rupture directivity, although different velocity mod els were used for location and moment tensor inversions. Our solution is similar to several other moment tensors from various agencies (Table S3); however, the scalar moments of the two global solutions (USGS ${ \bf { M } } _ { \mathrm { { w w } } }$ and gCMT) are 1.2–2.8 times larger than those of the three regional‐distance solutions. A similar dis crepancy was observed for the nearby 2017 $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ 5.3 Sulphur Peak, Idaho, earthquake (Koper et al., 2018) and may be related to the longer periods and higher near‐source rigidities used in the global solutions. Four of the five moment tensors have western dips of $3 4 { - } 3 8 ^ { \circ }$ for the presumptive fault plane, while the glo bal USGS solution $\left( \mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { w w } } \right)$ is an outlier with a western dip of $6 3 ^ { \circ } .$ . We attempted to calculate moment tensors for 34 larger aftershocks and were successful for eight of these events (Figures S5–S12), finding mechanisms similar to the mainshock with presumptive fault planes dipping west at $1 9 { - } 4 3 ^ { \circ }$ (Figure 1 and Table S4).

## 3.2. Spectral Modeling of the Mainshock

We modeled $\mathrm { S _ { g } }$ velocity spectra recorded on the transverse components on nearby stations to infer additional mainshock properties. We selected data from three‐component seismometers at distances of 0–300 km from the mainshock, resulting in 45 stations (35 broadband and 10 strong motion) from three networks (RE, US, and UU). The traces were converted to ground velocity and the horizontal components were rotated to radial and transverse. We used a 20‐s long time window on the transverse component that included $\mathrm { S _ { g } }$ and its early coda as the signal window and a 20‐s long window ending just before the theoretical P arrival as the noise window. The signal and noise windows were converted to the frequency domain using multitaper spectral processing software (Prieto et al., 2009). We used five quadratic multitapers and a bandwidth product of 3 to obtain smooth spectra with low leakage. The amplitude spectra were binned in $\log _ { 1 0 }$ space, and for each station, the usable frequency range was defined as the band including the spectral peak that had signal‐to noise ratios above 3.

We fit a Brune source model (Brune, 1970, 1971) to the velocity spectra using an iterative search method (Edwards et al., 2010). The three model parameters are the corner frequency (f<sub>c</sub>), the whole path anelastic attenuation operator (t\*), and the long‐period spectral offset $\left( \Omega _ { 0 } \right)$ . In an initial pass, we left all three parameters unconstrained. Because this is a moderate‐sized event, it is unlikely that the trade‐off between corner frequency and anelastic attenuation poses a significant problem; however, the measured (apparent) $\mathrm { f _ { c } }$ at a given station can vary because of directivity effects. To obtain a stable, azimuthally averaged event corner frequency, we took the $\log _ { 1 0 }$ average of all station estimates, preferring broadband measurements over strong motion when both sensor types were present. This process resulted in a value of $0 . 5 8 \pm 0 . 0 6$ Hz (95% confidence) for $\mathrm { f _ { c } } .$ We then treated this value as fixed and refit the observations with t\* and $\Omega _ { 0 }$ as free parameters. We used a recently developed geometrical spreading model for Utah (Holt, 2019) to convert $\Omega _ { 0 }$ to scalar moment $\left( \mathbf { M } _ { 0 } \right)$ with a near‐source density of $2 . 8 \ : \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 }$ and a near‐source shear velocity of 3.5 km/s. At each station, $\mathbf { M } _ { 0 }$ was converted to $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ and the median of these values (5.53) was selected as the event $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { w } }$ (Figure S13). Assuming a circular rupture, we obtain a fault radius of 2.3 km, a fault area of $1 6 \mathrm { k m } ^ { 2 } ,$ , an average slip of 0.28 m, and a stress drop of 8.3 MPa.

## 3.3. Template‐Based Detection of Aftershocks

To enrich the UUSS catalog with smaller events, we employed a matched filtering technique (e.g., Ross et al., 2019; Shelly, 2020) on all available seismic data within 40 km of the mainshock for the time period of 17 March 2020 (1 day before the mainshock) through 30 April 2020. There were 226 channels of data recorded at 39 seismograph stations. The data were subdivided into hour‐long windows that overlapped by 120 s. The windowed data were detrended, filtered with one pass of a two‐pole Butterworth 3–14 Hz band‐pass filter, and, if necessary, resampled in the Fourier domain to the nominal network sampling rate of 100 Hz. After preprocessing, we extracted P wave templates using a window of −0.2 to 1.6 s around the catalog picks and S templates using a window of −0.2 to 2.6 s around the catalog picks. If the catalog pick was made on a three‐component station, then waveform templates on all three channels were extracted. We prepared a total of 56,679 P wave templates and 30,491 S wave templates.

The hour‐long data segments were matched filtered with appropriate templates using an efficient process (Senobari et al., 2019), and detections with absolute correlation coefficients above 0.6 were retained. For a handful of stations, we increased the threshold to 0.75–0.90 so that the number of detections contributed by a channel did not exceed a few hundred thousand. To prevent multiple detections in the sidelobes, we required that two detections generated by any one template not be within 4 s of one another. Should two detections be within this window, the detection with the higher correlation coefficient was retained. We associated detections into unique events using DBSCAN (Ester et al., 1996). We required that the origin time estimates from a cluster of detections associated to a common event be within 0.3 s of one another and that each cluster have a minimum of five detections (either P or S). Origin times were assigned by an unweighted average of detection estimates. The standard deviation of origin time estimates was less than 1.03 samples for 75% of the events, and the largest standard deviation was 33 samples, implying that the detected events have hypocenters in close proximity to the reference template events. Finally, we required that no detected events were within $\pm 5$ s of one another or a catalog event. If multiple events were found within a 5‐s window, we selected the event whose sum of absolute correlation coefficients was greatest, so as to promote events that had been detected on more stations. This resulted in the identification of 5,501 new aftershocks and no fore shocks (Figure S14). An example detection is shown in Figure S15.

We assigned two magnitude estimates to each newly detected event. The first method estimates a scalar that provides the best fit between the detection and template (Gibbons & Ringdal, 2006). The $\log _ { 1 0 }$ of this value provides a perturbation to the template magnitude, and the perturbations from all the detections are averaged to generate the magnitude perturbation for the newly detected event. The second method essentially uses root mean square ratios between the template and detection to calculate magnitude perturbations (Schaff & Richards, 2014). The methods give the same result when the two waveforms are identical. We treat the first magnitude estimate as a lower bound and the second as an upper bound and average the two esti mates to define a preferred magnitude.

## 3.4. b Value Estimation

To monitor the potentially changing stress field following the mainshock, we calculated the b value time series ofthe ongoing sequence and compared it to the background value for the Salt Lake Valley area. Gulia and Wiemer (2019) proposed that time‐varying b value analysis can help identify whether ongoing earthquakes in a sequence are aftershocks of an earlier large event or foreshocks of a mainshock yet to occur. We experi mented with different catalog subsets, magnitude cut‐offs, and time windows as described in Text S3. Our methodology followed Gulia and Wiemer (2019) but with some variation, for instance in the use of a rela tively large area to calculate the background b value because of the low historical rate of seismicity in the Magna region. We found that in the hours following the mainshock, the b value did decrease from the back ground level of \~0.8 to \~0.4, potentially signaling that these events were foreshocks. However, within about 8 hr, the b value returned to near‐background levels of0.7–0.8 and remained in that range through the end of our analysis on 30 April (Figure S16). Our observations would be classified with a yellow rating in the Gulia and Wiemer (2019) traffic light system (b value mostly unchanged, with no additional discriminating information).

## 3.5. High‐Precision Relocation of Aftershocks

To image the fine structure of the fault system associated with the Magna sequence, we used waveform cross‐correlation to measure differential travel times and inverted these times using a hierarchical clustered relocation method to generate high‐precision relative hypocenters (Trugman & Shearer, 2017). We used a two‐stage process in which we first relocated the catalog events and then added the new template‐based detections with relatively low weights. Details of the procedure are given in Text S3, as are the results of various robustness tests which involve using different sets of stations, different criteria for accepting differential times, an alternative relocation algorithm, variable starting locations for the newly detected events, and so on (Tables S6 and S7 and Figures S17–S19). We were able to relocate 5,623 aftershocks. The mainshock and many of the larger aftershocks were not relocated because their relatively complicated waveforms were poorly correlated with those of the more numerous smaller aftershocks.

Most of the relocated aftershocks form a roughly planar, west‐dipping zone at depths of 8–10 km (Figure 3). Fitting a single plane to these events yields a strike of $1 5 9 ^ { \circ }$ and a dip of $2 1 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { W }$ with nominal uncertainties of

$1 { - } 2 ^ { \circ }$ . This orientation is slightly different than that of the mainshock fault plane in our preferred moment tensor (strike of $1 8 2 ^ { \circ }$ and dip of 34°W). It is possible that epistemic uncertainties can account for the discrepancy. For instance, if accurate 3D anisotropic velocity models were available for computing Green's func tions and travel times, variations of $5 { - } 1 0 ^ { \circ }$ in both planes might be expected; however, the thickness of the aftershock cluster (1–2 km) when viewed in cross section and the appearance of at least two separate planar substructures suggest that multiple faults, with varying orientations, were activated during the sequence. In Figure 3, a subcluster of 760 events (blue dots) north of the mainshock epicenter forms a plane with strike of $1 7 7 ^ { \circ }$ and dip of $3 2 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { W }$ , while a subcluster of 692 events (red dots) south of the epicenter forms a plane with strike of $1 2 6 ^ { \circ }$ and dip of $3 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { W }$ . There is also the hint of an upward, convex, curve to the base of the aftershock zone as one moves eastward from the red subcluster. No subclusters are observed dipping steeply east among these deeper aftershocks.

A smaller subset of 237 aftershocks occurred 5–6 km east of the main cluster at depths of $^ { 5 - 6 }$ km. These events form a roughly planar structure with a strike of $3 4 6 ^ { \circ }$ and dip of $6 3 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { E }$ and nominal uncertainties of $3 { - } 6 ^ { \circ }$ . There is some evidence for fault complexity in this cluster—perhaps a counterclockwise rotation in strike of $1 5 - 2 0 ^ { \circ }$ as one moves from south to north—but with far fewer events, the complexity is not as clear as in the western cluster. The upward extension of this plane aligns well with the surface traces of the mostly east‐dipping West Valley fault system. Though the near‐surface dips of West Valley faults are not well con strained, steep angle antithetic faults are commonly observed in the hanging walls of normal fault systems. This aftershock cluster began in earnest several days after the mainshock and may have been triggered by the static stress change from the mainshock.

To investigate this idea, we calculated the Coulomb stress changes (Lin & Stein, 2004; Toda et al., 2005) at 9‐km depth (centroid depth) on a fault plane with 5‐km length and 3‐km width. We used two different fault planes as receiver faults: (i) the plane corresponding to the main rupture with strike, dip, and rake (182/34/ −52) taken from the moment tensor solution calculated in this study (Table S3) and (ii) a fault plane appropriate for the eastern earthquake cluster with strike and dip estimated from the relocated seismicity (345°/ 64°/−52°) (Table S7). Modeling details and the corresponding map of the Coulomb failure function (ΔCFF, Reasenberg & Simpson, 1992) are presented in Figure S20. The eastern aftershock cluster is mostly located in an area with $\Delta \mathrm { C F F } > 0 . 1$ bar, sufficient to trigger off‐fault seismicity (King et al., 1994; Orlecka‐Sikora, 2010; Stein, 1999); hence, these aftershocks were likely triggered by stress changes from the Magna mainshock. This finding is consistent with paleoseismological data linking past seismic activity on the SLCS and the West Valley fault system (DuRoss & Hylland, 2015) and suggests that coseismic rupture on the West Valley fault system should be considered in future simulations of ground motion from large SLCS earthquakes.

## 4. Discussion and Conclusions

The ${ \bf { M } } _ { \mathrm { { w } } }$ 5.7 Magna earthquake and aftershock sequence resolves a complex, piece‐wise planar, and shallow west‐dipping normal fault zone consistent with the geometry and kinematics of the SLCS of the WFZ. Given that the dip of the northernmost portion of the SLCS is observed to be ${ \sim } 7 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { W }$ at the surface (Bruhn et al., 1992), our preferred model is a master listric SLCS—with dips of $2 0 { - } 3 0 ^ { \circ }$ reached at depths of 9– 10 km—overlain by a system of shallow‐dipping (subparallel) synthetic and steep‐angle antithetic faults (Figure 3), similar to patterns expressed in mechanical models (Bruhn & Schultz, 1996) and observed during analog experiments of normal fault formation (Bose & Mitra, 2009). The fault model presented in Figure 3 is not unique and it is possible, for instance, to give the deep fault a slightly shallower dip such that it intersects the mass of aftershocks projected on the cross section. However, we do consider a general listric model more likely than the alternative of a planar, steeply dipping SLCS underlying the Magna sequence by several kilometers because there are no local seismic observations to support such a model and synthetic faults in the hanging wall of a normal fault system commonly have steeper dips than the underlying master fault (e.g., Burbank & Anderson, 2011).

A listric fault geometry for the SLCS is consistent with surface observations (Bruhn et al., 1992), shallow seis mic reflection data (Smith & Bruhn, 1984; Velasco et al., 2010), and geodetic models (Bennett et al., 2007; Velasco et al., 2010) and contributes to the long‐standing debate on whether low‐angle (<30–35°) normal faults are seismogenic (Axen et al., 1999; Jackson, 1987; Wernicke, 1995). Although low‐angle norma fault initiation conflicts with Andersonian theory and few, if any, low‐angle normal‐faulting rupture planes are documented with seismologic data (Collettini & Sibson, 2001; Jackson, 1987; Jackson & White, 1989), geologic reconstructions, paleomagnetic and thermochronologic studies, and seismic reflection data provide evidence in support of active slip along low‐angle structures (Wernicke, 1995). The shallow dips of the west‐dipping nodal planes in the nine Magna moment tensors combined with the shallow west‐dipping patterns in the relocated Magna aftershocks support the idea that shallow‐dipping normal faults can fail seismically.

Our observations have direct implications for seismic hazard and the potential mitigation of seismic risk in the Salt Lake City metropolitan area. The shallow dip of the SLCS at depth may increase the area of rupture zones of future large earthquakes and bring them closer to the surface with correspondingly stronger ground shaking than what is predicted by current rupture models that assume a constant dip of \~50°. Furthermore, the Magna mainshock rupture area (\~16 km<sup>2</sup>) is much smaller than the estimated seismogenic area of the SLCS (\~800 km<sup>2</sup>), implying that a significant amount of strain remains to be released. Although it is unclear why only a portion of the SLCS ruptured, it is possible that the laterally discontinuous surface geometry of SLCS—if manifested at depth—played a role in limiting the rupture. The northernmost SLCS includes the \~10 km long Warm Springs fault (Figure 3), which steps 3–5 km east to the East Bench fault at its southern end and \~2 km east to the Weber segment at its northern end. Complex faulting at depth related to deformation in zones of stepping or overlapping faults could have impeded along‐strike propagation of the rupture (Bruhn et al., 1987; King & Nabelek, 1985). Regardless, given that the last major rupture on this part of the WFZ was \~1,400 years ago and that its recurrence interval is thought to be \~1,300 years (DuRoss et al., 2016), enough strain remains on the SLCS to produce an M7 earthquake.
