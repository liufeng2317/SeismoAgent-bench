# Rapid Characterization of the July 2019 Ridgecrest, California, Earthquake Sequence From Raw Seismic Data Using Machine‐Learning Phase Picker

Min Liu<sup>1,2</sup>, Miao Zhang<sup>2</sup> , Weiqiang Zhu<sup>3</sup>, William L. Ellsworth<sup>3</sup> , and Hongyi Li<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Geophysics and Information Technology, China University of Geosciences (Beijing), Beijing, China, <sup>2</sup>Department of Earth and Environmental Sciences, Dalhousie University, Halifax, Nova Scotia, Canada, <sup>3</sup>Department of Geophysics, Stanford University, Stanford, CA, USA

Abstract The two principle earthquakes of the July 2019 Ridgecrest, California, earthquake sequence, M<sub>W</sub> 6.4 and 7.1, and their immediate foreshocks and thousands of aftershocks present a challenging environment for rapid analysis and characterization of this sequence as it unfolded. In this study, we analyze the first 6 days of the sequence using continuous data from available seismic networks to detect and locate earthquakes associated with the earthquake sequence. We build a high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake catalog using a deep<sup>‐</sup>neural<sup>‐</sup>network<sup>‐</sup>based picker<sup>—</sup>PhaseNet and a sequential earthquake association and location workflow. Without prior information, we automatically detect and locate more than twice as many earthquakes as the routine catalog. Our high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake catalog reveals detailed spatiotemporal evolution of the earthquake sequence and clearly defines multiple faults activated during the sequence. Our study demonstrates that it is possible to characterize earthquake sequences from raw seismic data using a well<sup>‐</sup>trained machine<sup>‐</sup>learning picker and our workflow.

Plain Language Summary We build a high‐precision earthquake catalog for the July 2019 Ridgecrest, California, earthquake sequence from 4 July 2019 to 9 July 2019 without prior information using machine<sup>‐</sup>learning phase picks and a sequential earthquake association and location workflow. Our result is totally independent of the routine catalog and enables us to characterize the earthquake sequence starting from raw seismic data. Our high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake catalog reveals detailed spatiotempora evolution of the earthquake sequence and clearly defines multiple faults activated during the sequence.

## 1. Introduction

On 4 July 2019, seismic activity initiated near Ridgecrest, California, and escalated over the next 2 hr culminating with the occurrence of the first principle mainshock M 6.4 at 17:33 UTC. It was followed by an intense aftershock sequence leading up to the occurrence of the second mainshock, M 7.1 at 03:19 UTC on 6 July 2019. The M 6.4 and 7.1 mainshocks occurred closely in space (10<sup>‐</sup>km separation) and activated a complex fault network composed of conjugate strike<sup>‐</sup>slip faults including the NW<sup>‐</sup>trending main fault with about 65<sup>‐</sup>km<sup>‐</sup>long surface rupture and NE<sup>‐</sup>treading fault with \~15<sup>‐</sup>km<sup>‐</sup>long surface rupture (Barnhart et al., 2019; Goldberg et al., 2019; Kendrick et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019; Ross et al., 2019; Figure 1). A high<sup>‐</sup>precision near real<sup>‐</sup>time catalog enables us to rapidly characterize the earthquake sequence (Stewart, 1977), such as tracking seismicity evolution (Peng & Zhao, 2009; Ross et al., 2017; Zhang & Wen, 2015a) and identification of causative faults (Kao & Shan, 2007; Yang et al., 2009). The challenge is to build a high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake catalog in near real<sup>‐</sup>time when the event rate becomes extreme.

Earthquake catalog building strongly depends on earthquake detection and location techniques. Two main classes of methods are widely used in earthquake detection and location: pick<sup>‐</sup>based methods and waveform<sup>‐</sup>based methods (Grigoli et al., 2018; Pesicek et al., 2014; Stewart, 1977; Zhang et al., 2019). Independent of seismic phase picking, waveform<sup>‐</sup>based methods detect, associate, and locate earthquakes simultaneously in a single step based on the delay<sup>‐</sup>and<sup>‐</sup>sum concept (Kao & Shan, 2004; Peng & Zhao, 2009; Shelly et al., 2007; Zhang & Wen, 2015b). They are sensitive to small earthquakes by maximizing seismic energy or coherence but suffer from high computational costs due to their exhaustive searching in space and time. Consequently, pick<sup>‐</sup>based methods are still the preferred solution for routine earthquake monitoring due to their high computational efficiency (Grigoli et al., 2018). Seismic picks are conventionally obtained from handpicking or automatic picking algorithms such as the short<sup>‐</sup>term average/long<sup>‐</sup>term average picker (Allen, 1978; Withers et al., 1998). Recently, machine<sup>‐</sup>learning techniques have been widely adopted in seismic phase picking, showing promise in both picking efficiency and picking accuracy (e.g., Ross et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2019; Zhu & Beroza, 2019).

A high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake catalog provides a window into the details of seismic fault structures. Various techniques have been widely used to investigate fault structures after earthquakes occurred but hampered by different aspects, such as: Focal mechanism inversion methods are only eligible to reveal the major fault plane (e.g., Gephart & Forsyth, 1984); geodetic observations have little depth resolution (e.g., Ward & Valensise, 1989); and on<sup>‐</sup>site surveys are not able to investigate buried faults (e.g., Xu et al., 2009). Earthquakes may be accommodated on known or newly activated faults (Ross et al., 2019; Waldhauser, 2009; Waldhauser & Ellsworth, 2002). Thus, accurate earthquake locations play an important role in deli neation of detailed seismic fault structures.

In this study, we first build sequential earthquake catalogs for the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquake sequence from continuous waveform data using a machine<sup>‐</sup>learning picker, a newly developed earthquake association and location algorithm, and two other high<sup>‐</sup>precision location programs. Then we compare our catalogs with other available catalogs. Our results clearly reveal the seismicity migration in space and time and character ize seismic fault structures activated during the earthquake sequence.

## 2. High‐Precision Earthquake Catalog Building

The study region focuses on the fracture zone activated by the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 and $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshocks (Figure 1). From the first foreshock of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 on 4 July to the time of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1, the Southern California Seismic Network (SCSN) reported 1,708 earthquakes. Another 5,717 aftershocks occurred following the M 7.1 through 9 July 2019 for a total of 7,425 events. Continuous seismic data for 41 permanent stations and four temporary stations located within 120 km of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock are used in this study (Figure 1a and supporting information, Figure S1). We adopt the Coso regional 1<sup>‐</sup>D velocity model as our initial velocity model in the study, which was obtained from travel time inversion and well log (Feng & Lees, 1998).

Our workflow generally follows the one used by Zhang et al. (2019) who used a conventional short<sup>‐</sup>term average/long<sup>‐</sup>term average picker. In this study, a machine<sup>‐</sup>learning phase picker is used to build sequential earthquake catalogs for the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquake sequence. The whole process consists of three sequence steps: (1) phase detection and picking using the deep<sup>‐</sup>neural<sup>‐</sup>network<sup>‐</sup>based arrival<sup>‐</sup>time picker<sup>—</sup> PhaseNet (Zhu & Beroza, 2019); (2) phase association and initial event location using our newly developed rapid earthquake association and location method (REAL; Zhang et al., 2019); and (3) earthquake sequentia relocation using absolute and relative location algorithms (Kissling et al., 1994; Waldhauser & Ellsworth, 2000; Zhang et al., 2019). PhaseNet was trained on the prodigious dataset of analyst<sup>‐</sup>labeled P<sup>‐</sup> and S<sup>‐</sup>arriva times from over 30 years of earthquake recordings from the Northern California Seismic Network (Zhu & Beroza, 2019). PhaseNet independently identifies seismic P and S phases by calculating their probabilities from either vertical or three<sup>‐</sup>component waveforms. Here, we empirically set the probability threshold to 0.5 (see an example in Figure S2). REAL associates and initially locates earthquakes primarily through counting the number of P and S picks and secondarily from travel time residuals by grid searching (Zhang et al., 2019). In this study, the searched area is $0 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ in horizontal dimension with a grid size of $0 . 0 2 ^ { \circ }$ (about 1.7 km) and from the surface to 20<sup>‐</sup>km depth with a grid size of 2 km. The grid is centered at the station recording the initiating P phase. Seismic stations with epicentral distance of <100 km are used for earth quake association and location in this study. Only events with a sufficient number of associated seismic P and S phases will be retained, and their location will be preliminarily determined at the grid with the max imum number of seismic picks or the grid with the smallest travel time residual if multiple grids have the same most number of picks. We conservatively set a strict threshold of five P picks and a total of 13 P and S picks. We relocate these earthquakes using an absolute location algorithm<sup>—</sup>VELEST (Kissling et al., 1994) and further refine their locations using a double<sup>‐</sup>difference location method<sup>—</sup>hypoDD (Waldhauser & Ellsworth, 2000).

The REAL catalog contains 16,563 earthquakes from 4 July 2019 through 9 July 2019 (Figures S3 and S4). Associated phases consist of 195,869 arrival times of P waves and 204,349 arrival times of S waves. They fal nicely within the trend of the travel time curves for P and S waves of the local velocity model (Figure S5). Local magnitudes of all earthquakes are calculated based on a widely used amplitude<sup>‐</sup>and<sup>‐</sup>local<sup>‐</sup>magnitude relationship (Hutton & Boore, 1987; see the supporting information). We first select 1,633 events with at least 30 high<sup>‐</sup>quality P and S picks (pick probability >0.8) to update the aforementioned velocity model and esti mate station corrections using the VELEST. Subsequently, we refine the whole REAL catalog using the VELEST and the updated 1<sup>‐</sup>D velocity model with station corrections. We retain well<sup>‐</sup>constrained earth quakes with ${ < } 2 0 0 ^ { \circ }$ station gap and <0.6<sup>‐</sup>s travel time residual, resulting in 16,112 relocated earthquakes (Figures 2b, S6b, and S7). Lastly, we employ hypoDD and the updated 1<sup>‐</sup>D velocity model to further improve the locations of 15,445 events (Figures 2c, S6c, and S7). When applying the hypoDD program, we introduce three strategies to improve the location resolution: (1) we only use seismic stations with epicentral distance of <80 km; (2) we only use phase picks that best fit the major trend of P and S travel time curves (see the supporting information); and (3) we only use phase picks with probability of >0.7, and the pick probabilities are allocated as the corresponding phase weighting.

We compare the absolute location VELEST catalog and relative location hypoDD catalog with two other cor responding catalogs in the same time period and study region: SCSN routine catalog and its waveform cross correlation relocation catalog (routine CC catalog; see Acknowledgments). Our VELEST catalog lists more than twice as many events as the SCSN routine catalog but with 12% of events missing due to strict threshold settings in the workflow (see the supporting information). Compared with absolute location catalogs, the two relative location catalogs have higher resolution (Figures 2, S6, and S7). They both clearly outline the structures of the NW<sup>‐</sup>trending main fault, the NE<sup>‐</sup>trending fault, and some branch faults such as two major subparallel SE<sup>‐</sup>trending branch faults at the southeastern end of the main fault (see fault naming in Figure S8), which are consistent with the surveyed surface ruptures very well (Kendrick et al., 2019; Figures 2c and 2d). Our hypoDD catalog has comparable resolution with the routine CC catalog, resulting in a more detailed definition of structures such as multiple near<sup>‐</sup>orthogonal faults cutting through the NW<sup>‐</sup>trending main fault (Ross et al., 2019), due to both large number of events and precision of locations (Figure 2c).

## 3. Characterization of Seismicity Evolution and Seismically Defined Fault Structures

We investigate the detailed spatiotemporal evolution of the 2019 Ridgecrest sequence using the hypoDD cat alog. A brief, intense foreshock sequence initiated 2 hr before and very close to the hypocenter of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 mainshock (Figures 3a, S9a, and S10a). Aftershocks occurring within 10 min of the mainshock clearly define the L<sup>‐</sup>shaped fault structure that ruptured in the mainshock (Movie S1). The foreshocks and mainshock hypocenter locate to the northwest of the NE<sup>‐</sup>trending fault (Figure S10a), suggesting that either the NW trending branch ruptured first (Ross et al., 2019), which would unclamp the NE<sup>‐</sup>trending fault, or the initial rupture was on a small NE<sup>‐</sup>SW fault seen at the surface (Goldberg et al., 2019), implying a multiply cascading sequence of ruptures formed the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 earthquake. Unfortunately, resolution of the structure of the foreshock sequence falls just beyond the precision of the PhaseNet arrival time measurements, and so with these data, we cannot determine if the foreshocks align with the NW or NE nodal plane or have a more complex geometry.

Seismicity activated near the hypocenter of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 event about 5 hr after the M 6.4 mainshock (Figures S10c and S10d and Movie S1). Nine hours later, a burst of seismicity further extended activity towards the hypocenter of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock along the NW<sup>‐</sup>trending branch fault (Figures S10d and S10e and Movie S1). Although seismicity migrated along the trend of the NW<sup>‐</sup>trending branch fault that ruptured in the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 mainshock (Liu et al., 2019; Ross et al., 2019), the majority ofthese aftershocks locate on multi ple NE<sup>‐</sup>striking and NW<sup>‐</sup>dipping fault zones, approximately parallel to the NE<sup>‐</sup>trending fault (Figures 3b and S10e; Ross et al., 2019). Following the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock, aftershocks rapidly migrated along the whole main fault and filled the seismicity gap between the NW<sup>‐</sup>dipping fault zones and the hypocenter of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 mainshock in a depth range of 0<sup>–</sup>7 km (Figures 3c and S10f). We notice that, after the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } } 7 . 1$ mainshock, seismicity on orthogonal structures cut through the NW<sup>‐</sup>trending main fault (Figures 3c and 4). Seismicity on the NE<sup>‐</sup>trending fault ruptured in the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 mainshock decreased significantly after the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock (Figures S9b and S9c).

We observe triggered earthquake clusters during both aftershock sequences of the two mainshocks. Two minor clusters of triggered events followed the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 mainshock: about 25 km NW of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 mainshock (about 5<sup>‐</sup>hr delay) and 19.5 km SSE of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 mainshock (about 14<sup>‐</sup>hr delay; Figure S11). Two major clusters of triggered events followed the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock: north of the Coso geothermal field (abou 6<sup>‐</sup>hr delay) and about 25 km NE of its epicenter (about 4<sup>‐</sup>hr delay; Figure S12). Both clusters locate in regions of coulomb stress increase imparted by the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock (Toda & Stein, 2019).

We delineate detailed seismic structures from our high<sup>‐</sup>precision seismicity catalog (Figures 3 and S9 and Movie S2). Accumulated seismicity patterns clearly define the two widely reported conjugated faults (Goldberg et al., 2019; Ross et al., 2019; Stewart, 2019; Toda & Stein, 2019; Figure 3). The two mainshocks and many of their aftershocks align with their focal mechanisms. The $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 mainshock and its aftershocks clearly define the <sup>“</sup>L<sup>”</sup> shape fault system of NE<sup>‐</sup>trending and NW<sup>‐</sup>trending branch faults, as well as several of the prominent NE<sup>‐</sup>striking, NW<sup>‐</sup>dipping orthogonal faults (Figures 3b and 4). The $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock activated an extended and complex fault network, particularly at its NW and SE ends (Figures 3c and 4), such as two major subparallel SE<sup>‐</sup>trending branch faults and at least three major near<sup>‐</sup>orthogonal faults centered at the junction of the NW<sup>‐</sup>trending main fault and the NE<sup>‐</sup>trending fault in the depth range of 3<sup>–</sup>10 km, cut ting through the main fault, which were mainly activated after the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } } 7 . 1$ mainshock (Figures 4 and S13).

In addition to these structures, we observe a nearly aseismic zone in the Coso geothermal field, bounding both the northern extent of surface rupture and seismicity migration (Ross et al., 2019; Figure 2c). The location of the aseismic zone agrees remarkably well with the crustal magma chamber imaged by teleseismic tomography (Reasenberg et al., 1980). North of the Coso geothermal field, seismicity is concentrated at shal low depth (<8 km).

## 4. Discussion

Based on a well<sup>‐</sup>trained machine<sup>‐</sup>learning phase picker (Zhu & Beroza, 2019) and a sequential earthquake association and location workflow (consisting of phase association, event initial location, improved relocation, and hypoDD relocation; Zhang et al., 2019), we are able to build a high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake catalog from raw continuous seismic data rapidly. Clearly, template matching could further significantly increase the number of events, and waveform cross<sup>‐</sup>correlation can further improve the location resolution (Ross et al., 2019; Waldhauser, 2009); however, both currently require significant computational resources and depend on the content of the routine catalog. In this study, the entire processing of the 6<sup>‐</sup>day data can be done in minutes/hours on a high<sup>‐</sup>performance cluster (depends on equipped graphics processing units and central processing units). Our work demonstrates the potential to build a high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake cat alog in near real<sup>‐</sup>time using machine<sup>‐</sup>learning methods, bridging the gap between conventional catalogs and cross<sup>‐</sup>correlation derived locations

In recent studies, machine<sup>‐</sup>learning phase pickers have shown promise in both picking efficiency and picking accuracy (e.g., Ross et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2019; Zhu & Beroza, 2019). However, the preci sion and resolution of earthquake locations determined from those machine<sup>‐</sup>learning picks have not been well studied. In this study, we first build a high<sup>‐</sup>quality earthquake catalog for the 2019 Ridgecrest sequence using machine<sup>‐</sup>learning picks, which shows consistent spatial distribution with surface fault ruptures with comparable resolution to the routine CC catalog (Figures 2, S6, and S7). Our study demonstrates the effective ness and accuracy of building a high<sup>‐</sup>resolution earthquake catalog based on machine<sup>‐</sup>learning picks.

The high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake catalog enables us not only to characterize the complex fault structures but also to understand the underlying source mechanisms. We found multiple faults activated by the earthquake sequence that did not break the surface (Kendrick et al., 2019). These structures are at best ambiguous in the routine catalog and routine CC catalog (Figure 2). The aftershocks of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 event activated at least two major near<sup>‐</sup>orthogonal buried faults cutting through the NW<sup>‐</sup>trending branch fault (Figures 3b and S14). Aftershocks of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 mainshock also migrated towards the hypocenter of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock, sug gesting a static triggering mechanism for the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock (Goldberg et al., 2019). The $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } } 7 . 1$ mainshock and its aftershocks activated at least three major near<sup>‐</sup>orthogonal buried faults cutting through the NW<sup>‐</sup>trending main fault (Figure 4). 3<sup>‐</sup>D views of the hypoDD locations clearly show the fault planes of those orthogonal faults dip to NW (Figures 4 and S14). On the other hand, the seismicity on the NE<sup>‐</sup>trending of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 6.4 rupture declined significantly after the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } } 7 . 1$ mainshock (Figures S9b and S9c), possibly suggesting it fell in a stress shadow of the $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { W } }$ 7.1 mainshock (Toda & Stein, 2019).

## 5. Conclusions

We constructed a high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake catalog for the July 2019 Ridgecrest, California, earthquake sequence from 4 July 2019 to 9 July 2019 without prior knowledge of the sequence. Our result is inde pendent of the routine catalog and enables us to characterize the earthquake sequence starting from continuous seismic data. We detect and locate more than twice as many events (15,445 events in hypoDD catalog) as the routine catalog (7,743) despite stringent association criteria (e.g., minimum of 13 arrival times). We investigate seismicity migration, fault structures, and triggered earthquakes asso ciated with the 2019 earthquake sequence from our high<sup>‐</sup>precision earthquake catalog.
