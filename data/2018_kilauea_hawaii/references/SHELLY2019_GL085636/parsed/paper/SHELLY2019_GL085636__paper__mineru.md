# Anatomy of a Caldera Collapse: Kīlauea 2018 Summit Seismicity Sequence in High Resolution

David R. Shelly<sup>1</sup> and Weston A. Thelen

<sup>1</sup>Geological Hazards Science Center, U.S. Geological Survey, Golden, CO, USA, <sup>2</sup>Cascades Volcano Observatory, U.S. Geological Survey, Vancouver, WA, USA

Abstract The 2018 Kīlauea eruption and caldera collapse generated intense cycles of seismicity tied to repeated large seismic $( M _ { w } \sim 5 )$ collapse events associated with magma withdrawal from beneath the summit. To gain insight into the underlying dynamics and aid eruption response, we applied waveform<sup>‐</sup> based earthquake detection and double<sup>‐</sup>difference location as the eruption unfolded. Here, we augment these rapid results by grouping events based on patterns of correlation<sup>‐</sup>derived phase polarities across the network. From April 29 to August 6, bracketing the eruption, we used \~2,800 events cataloged by the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory to detect and precisely locate 44,000+ earthquakes. Resulting hypocentroids resolve complex, yet coherent structures, concentrated at shallow depths east of Halema'uma'u crater, beneath the eventual eastern perimeter of surface collapse. Based on a preponderance of dilatational P wave first motions and similarities with previously inferred dike structures, we hypothesize that failure was dominated by coupled shear and crack closure.

Plain Language Summary We used high‐resolution methods to examine seismicity associated with the 2018 Kīlauea eruption and summit collapse, as magma drained underground from the summit region and eventually erupted toward the east. In total, we detected and precisely located more than 44,000 earthquakes from late April to early August 2018. The location patterns of these earthquakes reveal numerous seismically active structures, both on the boundary and within the collapsing zone. Based on the characteristics of these earthquakes, we hypothesize that most events were generated by a combination of fault slip and crack closure as the surface rocks collapsed into the underground region previously occupied by magma.

## 1. Introduction

The 2018 eruption of Kīlauea volcano was the finale of almost‐continuous eruption that began in 1983. For decades, scientists have recognized a close association between the summit and rift zones of shield<sup>‐</sup>stage Hawaiian volcanoes. In 2018 this association was fully illustrated between an enormous outpouring of lava in the lower East rift zone and the concurrent collapse of the summit of Kīlauea almost 40 km away. The caldera collapse occurred incrementally between May and early August 2018 through repeated events with subsidence on the scale of meters and typically associated with $M _ { w }$ 5.2<sup>–</sup>5.4 earthquakes (Butler, 2019; Neal et al., 2019; Segall et al., 2019). The collapse events punctuated a cycle where hundreds to thousands of smal ler earthquakes occurred within the deforming block (Shiro et al., 2018). The cumulative collapse dropped the crater floor by up to 500 m and enlarged the existing Halema'uma'u Crater by over 1,500 m in diameter. The eruption was recorded with a modern multiparameter monitoring network, including seismic, infra sound, geodetic (including GPS, InSAR, and LiDAR), and geochemical (gas and magma) analyses, made through ground and air<sup>‐</sup>based instruments (Neal et al., 2019). The resulting data sets provide the most com prehensive record of caldera collapse to date.

The mechanics of caldera collapse is a topic of considerable interest in volcanology. Because of the difficulty in capturing this process as it unfolds, much of what is known about caldera collapse has been learned from a combination of geological investigation of older, sometimes exhumed examples (e.g., Lipman, 1976), and from laboratory studies of analog materials (e.g., Roche et al., 2000). Field observations of eruptions of Fernandina (1968), Pinatubo (1991), Miyakejima (2000), Piton de la Fournaise (2007), and Bardarbunga (2014) volcanoes have all provided important constraints on caldera collapse processes (Kumagai et al., 2001; Michon et al., 2009; Mori et al., 1996; Riel et al., 2015; Stix & Kobayashi, 2008), yet none of these eruptions were captured by continuously recorded near<sup>‐</sup>field instrumentation approaching the quality avail able for the 2018 eruption of Kīlauea.

In this study, we focus on the Kīlauea summit collapse sequence. Summit microseismicity was incredibly prolific during the collapse cycles, making manual analysis (picking P and S phases) of the detected earthquakes largely impossible during the eruption. Additionally, routine location uncertainties were large enough to obscure structures within the relatively compact summit seismicity region. To address these issues, we used catalog events as waveform templates, combining event detection with precise relative relocation. A portion of this processing was performed in a <sup>“</sup>rapid research<sup>”</sup> response mode. Updated results were posted to the Hawaii Volcano Observatory (HVO) internal logs approximately weekly during the most active period of summit seismicity in June and early July (Table S1 in the supporting information), which were used by HVO personnel to aid with eruption interpretation and response.

Here, we present high<sup>‐</sup>resolution seismicity for 29 April to 6 August 2018, encompassing the period of most active seismicity at Kīlauea summit. We combine this result with phase polarity analysis and interpretations developed over the ensuing months. This seismicity was concentrated at quite shallow depths (<2.5 km below Kīlauea summit), notably shallower than zones of long‐period earthquakes (Matoza et al., 2014), mantle<sup>‐</sup>fault<sup>‐</sup>zone earthquakes, and tremor (Aki & Koyanagi, 1981; Wech & Thelen, 2015), which together reflect the deeper magmatic systems 5–60+ km beneath Kīlauea.

## 2. Methods

To enhance the seismic catalog, we followed the integrated earthquake detection and precise location method described by Shelly, Ellsworth, and Hill (2016). Full details are provided in the supporting information (Dawson et al., 1999; Lin et al., 2015). We used waveforms of 2,823 earthquakes cataloged by HVO (USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory [HVO], 1956; Shiro et al., 2018) as waveform templates spanning 3+ months from 29 April to 6 August 2018. During the month of May (Run 1; see Table S1), we used both automatic and analyst<sup>‐</sup>refined events as templates, but we later switched to only incorporating analyst<sup>‐</sup> refined events due to their higher quality and increasing numbers. In total, 44,188 Kīlauea summit earthquakes from 29 April to 6 August 2018 retained at least 10 P and 10 S correlation<sup>‐</sup>derived differential times after the hypoDD inversion (Waldhauser & Ellsworth, 2000) and are described here (Figure 1; Shelly, 2019).

We estimated magnitudes of newly detected events following the approach of Shelly, Ellsworth, and Hill (2016), based on the waveform amplitude ratios with HVO catalog earthquakes and their associated catalog magnitudes. However, during our analysis, we noted broad scatter in measured amplitude ratios and reported magnitude differences for pairs of HVO catalog events. We also noted a systematic bias for larger individual station HVO magnitude estimates from near<sup>‐</sup>source stations, perhaps as an artefact of complex structure in this area. Therefore, we suggest future work to refine HVO catalog magnitudes before detailed analysis of these magnitudes (such as b values) is undertaken. In this study, we only used magnitudes for coarse conveyance of approximate event sizes in figures.

To further evaluate events, we applied the correlation<sup>‐</sup>based phase polarity grouping as described by Shelly, Hardebeck, et al. (2016), repurposing the same correlation measurements as used for event detection and location. This technique uses the sign of the maximum absolute value waveform cross correlation as a measure of the relative polarity between like phases on a given data channel for a pair of earthquakes. Combining all 25 million such measurements, we grouped events into 50 hierarchical clusters of events with the most similar patterns of phase polarities across the network (Shelly, Hardebeck, et al., 2016; Shelly, 2019). Although differences in source location will affect polarities at some stations, this technique provides a systematic approach to distinguish event groups with different mechanisms within a particular source region. At this stage, given the complexity, we did not estimate focal mechanisms for the event groups.

## 3. Results and Discussion

## 3.1. Spatial Distribution of Seismicity

Figure 1 shows the resulting high<sup>‐</sup>resolution seismicity locations calculated for this study, spanning 29 Apri through 6 August 2018. Seismicity is concentrated beneath the eastern portion of the collapse zone, with comparatively little microseismicity in the western half. The eastern extent of seismicity largely aligns at or just beyond the perimeter of surface collapse, showing a near<sup>‐</sup>vertical boundary in cross section. The NE corner of seismicity extends farthest (up to several hundred meters) beyond the surface collapse. The distribution of seismicity is highly complex, with numerous concentrations of seismicity along the perimeter of the collapse but also numerous seismic structures interior to the collapse. A tendency for seismic structures to align in the WSW<sup>‐</sup>ENE direction is subparallel to the volcanic southwest rift zone and some fissure alignments within the caldera and may reflect local stress conditions (Poland et al., 2014). It also generally aligns with the fast direction of S wave anisotropy within the Kīlauea caldera, perhaps suggesting a structural influence (Johnson et al., 2015).

## 3.2. Onset of Summit Seismicity

For at least a month prior to the start of the 2018 eruption, shallow seismicity under the summit of Kīlauea had been elevated in the presence of high lava lake levels, indicating pressurization of the summit magma system (Klein et al., 1987; Patrick et al., 2015). As the dike propagated from Pu'u 'Ō'ō downrift to the lower East Rift Zone and eventually erupted on 4 May (UTC), deflationary tilt accelerated in the summit along with the rate of shallow summit earthquakes and the amplitude of tremor. These observations are consistent with depressurization of the summit system and an increase of magmatic flux downrift to the eruption site (Anderson et al., 2019). Deflationary tilt, and by inference depressurization, dominated for the rest of the eruption.

Summit seismicity increased markedly on 5 May (UTC; Figure 3), approximately 9 hr after the $M _ { w } 6 . 9$ earthquake that accommodated southward motion of the south flank (Figure 1 inset; Liu et al., 2018). Interestingly, the alignment of seismicity locations (dark green in Figures 2 and 3) suggests activation of a steeply inward dipping collapse<sup>‐</sup>bounding fault. Seismicity in this location was perhaps triggered by underlying magma evacuation and/or pressure reduction, as it occurred beneath the eastern margin of eventual surface collapse. This seismicity preceded the overlying surface expression of collapse by several weeks, with surface collapse reaching a similar eastward extent in late June.

## 3.3. Relationship of Seismicity to Collapse Cycles

Temporal patterns of microseismicity closely tracked cycles of (typically) $M _ { w } 5 . 2 – 5 . 4$ collapse events (Figure 3). To varying degrees, a pattern of seismic quiescence followed collapse events; following the quiescence, seismicity then ramped up to a high, sustained level until the next collapse event. A representative helicorder plot illustrating this cycle is shown in Figure S1. Notably, these patterns were not uniform among all seismicity sources nor were they constant over the evolution of the eruption (Figure 3). Figure S2 contrasts the seismicity patterns during the 6 hr before versus the 6 hr after collapse events. Seismicity rates were low shortly after collapse events, with events concentrated toward the center of the active region. In contrast, late in the cycle, while earthquakes continued in the central zone, seismicity increased markedly on the perimeter of the active zone.

These patterns evolved over the course of the sequence. For example, seismicity at 1<sup>‐</sup> to 2<sup>‐</sup>km depth beneath the eastern caldera began during the early part of the sequence in May, yet the shallower zone above this at less than 1<sup>‐</sup>km depth (red events in Figures 2 and 3) did not emerge until June, well after tremor noise declined in mid<sup>‐</sup>May. By late June these shallowest earthquakes became larger and started to exhibit quiescent and active periods synced to the cycle of larger collapse events (Figure S3), likely reflecting the development and linking of these faults to the surface. This timing was roughly coincident with overall stabilization of shallow seismic velocities in the area, perhaps also reflecting the maturation of these bounding faults (Hotovec<sup>‐</sup>Ellis et al., 2019).

A puzzling aspect of the seismicity observed here is its asymmetry in relation to the collapse zone, with seismicity concentrated in the eastern hemisphere of collapse (Figure 1). Although relatively lacking in seismicity overall, the western bounding fault may have slipped primarily during large collapse events and perhaps also aseismically. While a definitive explanation is currently lacking, the seismic behavior may be correlated with different histories of faulting bounding the collapse. On

the western edge, collapse seems to be bounded by the main caldera bounding fault. The bound on the south may also be a long<sup>‐</sup>lived fault, which perhaps also bounded the previous episode of summit collapse in \~1500 CE (Gailler et al., 2019; Gailler & Kauahikaua, 2018; Neal & Lockwood, 2003). In contrast, the eastern edge of collapse may have forced development of new faults. The immaturity of the eastern faults may be related to their greater seismogenesis.

## 3.4. Dilatational First Motions and Relationship to Magmatic Structures

Seismicity in this sequence exhibited a strong majority of dilatational P wave first motions, such as the example event shown in Figure 4a. Overall, the HVO catalog for the template events used here contains 10,321 (33%) compressional P wave first motions and 21,396 (67%) dilatational first motions. Further, this behavior does not segment clearly among the phase polarity clusters (Figure 2); rather, it is an overarching feature of the summit seismicity sequence. This attribute clearly merits a more detailed follow<sup>‐</sup>up analysis, yet we would be remiss not to mention it here. The dominance of dilatational first motions strongly suggests that earthquakes tend to exhibit mechanisms with negative isotropic components (volume reductions), such as would be expected from crack closure (Julian et al., 1998; Mildon et al., 2016). This is perhaps unsurprising given the degree of collapse at Kīlauea summit, and we expect that both horizontal (sill) and vertical (dike) cracks are commonly closing during the sequence, as magma depressurizes within these features (or creates a sill<sup>‐</sup>like void at the top of a magma chamber). There is ample evidence from gravity measurements for magma filled cracks and void space under much of Kīlauea caldera (Bagnardi et al., 2014; Johnson et al., 2010; Poland & Carbone, 2016). The strong seismic energy of many of these observed earthquakes and the variable mixture of dilatational and compressional first motions suggest a model where crack closure is coupled to shear failure in a hybrid mechanism (Figure 4b), as envisioned by Shimizu et al. (1987).

A subset of the seismicity away from likely bounding faults (interior to the collapse zone) relates to known or previously hypothesized shallow magmatic structures. For example, seismicity was localized almost directly beneath the former overlook vent in early May (depths of \~300–600 m below Kīlauea summit; Figure 2b), as the lava lake was rapidly dropping (Neal et al., 2019). These events may reflect shear faulting/collapse into the vent conduit as magma was withdrawn and/or magma pressure in the conduit decreased. Seismicity also aligns to form structures similar to previously proposed dike structures (Chouet & Dawson, 2011), including a near<sup>‐</sup>vertical structure striking toward the NE (the yellow events on the eastern edge of former Halema'uma'u shown in Figure 2a). In addition, the pink events on the SW side of Halema'uma'u crater occur directly below the surface expression of the volcanic Southwest Rift Zone, a shallow dike that has fed past eruptions to the SW of the Kīlauea Crater (Poland et al., 2014) and a major feature of the Chouet and Dawson (2011) model for very long period earthquake generation. As proposed above, seismicity might be generated by hybrid shear and crack closure induced by repeated depressurization ofthese dike structures as magma withdraws between collapse events.

## 3.5. Conceptual Model for Repeating Cycle of Seismicity and Collapse

Figure 4c summarizes our proposed conceptual model. We suggest that the initial state of Kīlauea volcano includes a magma body with distributed dikes (and sills) in its upper reaches (Stage 1). Although the degree to which dikes and sills populate the upper magma storage is debated, shallow magma conduits are sup ported by both direct (i.e., the visible lava lake and its conduit) and indirect (e.g., dikes inferred from waveform modeling of Chouet & Dawson, 2011) evidence. As magma flows out toward the east rift zone, these dikes and sills begin to depressurize. As they depressurize (Stage 2), they begin to close progressively, often in combination with shear slip on one or more faults emanating from the tip of the dike or sill (Stage 3). As magma continues to withdraw, faults bounding the slumping block also begin to slip (Stage 4). Finally, a large $( M _ { w } \sim 5 . 3 )$ ) collapse event slips the entire bounding fault. The gravitational force of the slumped block then suddenly repressurizes the system, reopening dikes (perhaps accompanied by reversed slip on asso ciated shear faults) and resetting the cycle.

This model is clearly highly idealized and neglects evolution over the course of the eruption, including the overall withdrawal of magma that eventually led to eruption cessation. In actuality, evolution over time was reflected in evolving character of collapse events, expansion of collapse extent, and development fault struc tures and associated seismicity. Furthermore, early collapse events were associated with ash emissions, which died out as the eruption progressed. We hypothesize a <sup>“</sup>stomp rocket<sup>”</sup> model. A stomp rocket is a toy where sudden compression (stomping) of an air bladder sends the air through an upward<sup>‐</sup>oriented tube forcing an attached <sup>“</sup>rocket<sup>”</sup> skyward. In this case, the collapsing roof block piston is the <sup>“</sup>stomp<sup>”</sup> that pressurized the gases between it and the magma column; these gases were then expelled through the still<sup>‐</sup> permeable conduit that earlier fed the summit lava lake carrying ash into the air (the <sup>“</sup>rocket<sup>”</sup>). This mode is supported by the presence of dilatational infrasound arrivals accompanying the start of the collapse, with an explosion<sup>‐</sup>like infrasound signal following seconds later (Thelen et al., 2018). Later, as this conduit became compacted and clogged with debris, ash ejections died out.

In the early stage of the eruption (i.e., the month of May), much of the deformation appears to have been accommodated at the surface by downwarping, with only a localized area of collapse at the surface near Halema'uma'u crater. Only later did fault structures develop to define a larger piston, extending to the east ern edge of seismicity (Figure 1). Even in these later stages, seismicity remained active internal to the piston, which (as discussed above) we hypothesize was generated by a mix of shear failure and crack closure.

## 3.6. Comparison With Other Calderas and Laboratory Models

Among the relatively few historical examples of caldera collapse, both Fernandina (1968; Simkin & Howard, 1970; Filson et al., 1973) and Miyakejima (2000; Kumagai et al., 2001) exhibited prolonged, incremental col lapse sequences (Stix & Kobayashi, 2008) qualitatively similar to that observed at Kīlauea. The collapse at Piton de la Fournaise volcano in 2007 was characterized by similar, but more rapid deformation cycles (Michon et al., 2009). Fernandina exhibited large seismicity at regular 6<sup>‐</sup>hr intervals (perhaps tidally modu lated; Filson et al., 1973), while very long period events were observed at Miyakejima once or twice per day (Kumagai et al., 2001). Despite these tantalizing similarities to repeated $\mathrm { M } _ { \mathrm { w } } \sim 5$ collapse events that punctu ated the 2018 Kīlauea sequence, we are largely unable to compare patterns of seismicity in greater detail; Fernandina had no local seismic recordings, while Miyakejima had a single temporary seismic station on the island, with remaining seismometers located more than 50 km away on the main Japanese island of Honshu (Kumagai et al., 2001).

Although idealized examples of caldera collapse might show a coherent <sup>“</sup>piston<sup>”</sup> falling along a simple ring fault, realistic examples are much more complex. For example, laboratory and field studies commonly describe a mix of outward dipping (reverse) and inward dipping (normal) fault structures, both at the mar gins of the collapse zone (i.e., the <sup>“</sup>ring fault<sup>”</sup>) and interior to the collapsing roof (i.e., the <sup>“</sup>piston<sup>”</sup>; e.g., Branney & Acocella, 2015; Cole et al., 2005; Roche et al., 2000). Lineaments in the relocated Kīlauea seismi city support the presence of structures within the collapsing block oriented to support both normal and reverse shear failure (see Movies S1 and S2), similar to structures observed in laboratory studies. The surface expression of the 2018 collapse zone expanded over the course of the eruption but was ultimately limited to only a portion of the overall caldera. During the initial stage of activity in May, much of the collapse appeared to occur in <sup>“</sup>downsag<sup>”</sup> mode (e.g., Acocella, 2007; Cole et al., 2005), where faults accommodating the volume loss beneath the summit had not yet reached the surface. Later, by early June, faults propagated to the surface outlining a smaller piston structure, while the eastern portion remained in downsag mode. Finally, in late June, the eastern fault structures propagated to the surface, outlining a larger piston. As this eastern fault fully developed, corresponding shallow seismicity became increasingly intense (Figure S3; Movie S1), likely reflecting slip over an expanded fault area

## 4. Conclusions and Future Work

The combination of a modern, dense network of seismometers with high resolution processing presented here illuminates seismicity associated with caldera collapse in unprecedented detail. This analysis reveals seismicity both on the perimeter of the collapsing roof block and within it, forming complex yet coherent seismic structures. Some of this complexity is similar to that observed in analog laboratory models. Structures internal to the block may reflect combined shear and crack closure, with some of these structures following previously inferred dike orientations. A dominance of dilatational P wave first motions supports this model. Seismicity is modulated by cycles of larger collapse events that may repeatedly repressurize the system, even while magma withdrawal continues at depth.

The wealth of data collected during and after the 2018 eruption will provide a treasure trove for analysis for years and even decades to come, revealing processes that controlled the eruption and the implications for future activity of the volcano. The analysis presented here leaves substantial opportunities for future investigation, including earthquake magnitudes, effects of ongoing seismic velocity changes, and perhaps most critically, earthquake mechanisms. We intend for this manuscript to provide a useful launching point for these future investigations.
